<Commentary>

If any part of the Enoch material in Moses has entered Latter-day Saint devotion on its own strength, it is the passage this installment is built around. Enoch asks the question a reader of classical theology is not supposed to be able to ask: *How is it that thou canst weep, seeing thou art holy, and from all eternity to all eternity?*

**Why the question matters.** Nibley's parenthetical — that the concept was "quite inadmissible to the Fathers of the fourth century" — is not a throwaway. Divine impassibility, the doctrine that God cannot suffer or be moved by creaturely grief, was close to settled among the theologians who inherited Greek philosophical categories, and it is precisely the fourth-century generation Nibley has been blaming since Part 1 for burying the book of Enoch. A God who weeps over his children is exactly the sort of thing such a book would have contained, and exactly the sort of thing that would have made it unwelcome.

**The parallel he finds for it.** The strongest single item is the passage he cites from the Jewish Encyclopedia, from the Babylonian Talmud: God weeping over the destruction of the temple, with Metatron — who in that literature *is* Enoch, translated and transformed — falling on his face and offering to weep in God's place, and God answering, "If thou wilt not suffer me to weep, I will go whither thou canst not come and there I will lament." The pairing is remarkable: not merely a weeping God, but a weeping God and the Enoch-figure, in the same scene, arguing over who is permitted to grieve.

Beside it Nibley sets the other half of the tableau — <VerseRef book="moses" chapter={7} verse="26" />, where Satan looks up and laughs while his angels rejoice, against the Slavonic Enoch's "the Adversary will be in his glory and rejoice in their works, to *my* great affliction." Grief in heaven and laughter in hell, in both texts, at the same moment.

**A philological detail worth catching.** The exchange about Noah in <VerseRef book="moses" chapter={7} verse="48-51" /> — the earth asking "when shall I rest," followed immediately by the plea for Noah's children — sits beside the Chester Beatty text, where Enoch tells Lamech to "call his name Noah, for he shall be a remnant whereon ye shall rest for a season." Both turn on the Hebrew pun between *Noah* and *nuah*, to rest. A wordplay that works only in Hebrew, appearing in both an ancient Greek Enoch papyrus and an English text dictated in 1830, is a more particular thing than a shared motif, and Nibley might have made more of it than he does.

**Where to apply the brakes.** Two places.

The catastrophe material in "World in Upheaval" leans on the state of the sciences in the mid-1970s and on a catastrophist reading that was fashionable then and is not now. Pole-shift, sudden tectonic upheaval, and meteoric bombardment as the background of the flood narratives are speculative framings, not findings, and his line that "the scientists rather than the theologians" are impressed by apocalyptic should be read as advocacy. The ancient texts do describe cosmic upheaval in vivid terms; what caused it, and whether anything did, is not settled by their vividness.

The second is subtler and more instructive. Faced with the list of tribes in <VerseRef book="moses" chapter={7} verse="9" /> — Sharon, Omner, Heni, Shem, Haner, Hananiah — which has no counterpart in the ancient sources, Nibley proposes that Ethiopic scribes replaced the unfamiliar names with Parthians and Medes. That converts an absence of correspondence into evidence for it. It may even be what happened; but an argument that can absorb both matches and mismatches has stopped being a test, and the series was built on the promise of a test.

</Commentary>

*In the previous installment, Brother Nibley painted a graphic portrait of Enoch’s world, a culture of immense sophistication and material prosperity whose people had a profound knowledge of the truth, yet deliberately perverted that knowledge, broke their covenants, married unbelievers, and mocked the Lord. This portrait is constructed from the book of Moses’ accounts of Enoch’s experiences and also of Moses’ experiences where they paralleled that early patriarch’s. The powerful comparisons with the experiences of Enoch and others that are reported in apocryphal literature show again the Prophet Joseph Smith’s inspiration in relating an account that so closely parallels undeniably ancient—though not necessarily scriptural—accounts of Enoch. Brother Nibley, in concluding his discussion of Enoch’s world and beginning a description of the cataclysms of nature that parallel the moral confusion of the people, stresses that they had deliberately chosen wickedness.*

## World in Upheaval

An unfailing aspect of Apocalyptic literature in general and of the Enoch writings in particular is the reverberation through their pages of vast upheavals in the natural world. This aspect of apocalyptic has begun to be taken seriously only within very recent years, and it is the scientists rather than the theologians who are impressed by the ancient records.[^(332)](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/ensign/1977/02/a-strange-thing-in-the-land-the-return-of-the-book-of-enoch-part-9?lang=eng#note332) Enoch, in fact, is one of their favorite references. They are impressed by the authentic ring of the catastrophic motif in the old apocalyptic writings while the ministry deplores and denounces them as unfortunate examples of the infantile scare-tactics of a bogeyman mentality.[^(333)](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/ensign/1977/02/a-strange-thing-in-the-land-the-return-of-the-book-of-enoch-part-9?lang=eng#note333)
