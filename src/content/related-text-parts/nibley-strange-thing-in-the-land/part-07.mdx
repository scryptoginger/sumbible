<Commentary>

Everything before this has been preparation. Here Nibley lays the texts out in columns and lets them face each other, and whatever one concludes about the series as a whole, this is the installment that has to be reckoned with.

**The Apocalypse of Abraham.** The choice of text matters enormously. This is a Slavonic apocalypse discovered in 1895 and first published by Bonwetsch in 1897 — sixty-seven years after Joseph Smith dictated Moses 1, and beyond any question of access. Nibley also notes that Klaus Koch ranks it among the small number of genuinely early Hebrew apocalypses, which matters because it answers the objection that dogged the 2 Enoch parallel in Part 6: here the source is both unavailable *and* plausibly ancient.

What Nibley shows is not a handful of shared images but a shared sequence. The hero is taken to a high mountain. He is promised a vision of the ages and of all the children of men. The presence withdraws and he collapses to the earth without strength. At precisely that moment the adversary appears. The hero asks "Who art thou?" The adversary is named and shamed by contrast with the hero, and — this is the detail worth stopping on — the garment of glory that once fitted Satan in heaven is said to be laid up for the hero instead, while Satan inherits the decay that was to have been the hero's. Then the hero is strengthened, commands Satan to depart, is borne aloft, is shown the whole earth and its numberless inhabitants, asks why these things are so, and is finally set back down alone on the earth.

That order is the argument. Individual motifs — mountains, visions, tempters — are common property in religious literature. A sequence of a dozen specific episodes in the same arrangement is a different kind of claim, and Nibley is right to rest his case here rather than on any single resemblance.

**Read the rows, not just the tables.** The alignment is Nibley's own, and it is not uniformly tight. Some rows pair genuinely equivalent material; others are looser than the layout suggests. The "many waters" row is the clearest example — <VerseRef book="moses" chapter={1} verse="25" /> has Moses made "stronger than many waters," meaning the Red Sea he will command, while Apocalypse of Abraham 17:1 has a voice *like* the sound of many waters, a stock theophany image. Placed side by side in a table they look like a match; read in context they are doing unrelated work. Several rows in the second half are of this kind. The strong rows remain strong, but the table format flatters the weak ones, and a careful reader should grade them individually rather than counting them.

**The Combat of Adam and Eve.** The second table is weaker evidence and Nibley half-concedes it, remarking drily that "the monkish mind could not resist the temptation to work a good thing to death." The text survives in late Ethiopic and Arabic manuscripts, and its content is transparently Christian — the closing scene has Satan wound Adam in the side while he prays, blood and water flowing onto the altar, with God explaining that he too will be pierced and that blood and water will come from his side. That is not an independent ancient witness converging on Joseph Smith; it is Christian typology, and its resemblance to <VerseRef book="moses" chapter={5} verse="6-10" /> tells us that both texts stand in a tradition of reading Adam's sacrifice as a similitude of the atonement.

Nibley's own framing at the head of the installment is the right one and worth keeping in view: these accounts are not scripture, only ancient records that help make sense of the Enoch story. The Apocalypse of Abraham parallels are the ones that carry weight, and they are strong enough that they do not need the others.

</Commentary>

The preceding installment was all familiar literary ground in our story’s great prologue, and that with a minimum of biblical prompting. Those who wish to credit Joseph Smith with a comprehension of comparative literature and ritual far beyond his time and training are free to do so. They may even insist, as they have with the Book of Mormon, that this is the way any uneducated rustic would tell the story. Today, however, we have several very ancient and significant parallels to [Moses 1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/pgp/moses/1?lang=eng), which lie far beyond the reach of coincidence or daydreaming. The number of details and the order in which they occur make it perfectly clear that we are dealing with specific works of great antiquity which come from a common source. To show what we mean, let us compare Moses’, Abraham’s, and Adam’s confrontations with Satan; these stories themselves contain pointed references to Enoch, with whom each hero is duly compared. Let it be remembered that these accounts are not scripture, but are simply ancient records that help us understand the Enoch story.

First the Apocalypse of Abraham, an Old Slavonic account discovered in 1895 and first published by Bonwetsch in 1897. K. Koch has recently ranked it as one of the five definitely authentic early Hebrew Apocalypses. Let us place it in parallel columns against our book of Moses, chapter 1.

These parallel accounts, separated by centuries, cannot be coincidence. Nor can all the others. The first man to have such a confrontation with Satan was Adam. A wealth of stories about it closely matches the accounts of Abraham. Moses, Enoch, and other heroes. Perhaps the oldest Adam traditions are those collected from all over the ancient East at a very early time, which have reached us in later Ethiopian and Arabic manuscripts under the title of “The Combat of Adam and Eve against Satan.” It contains at least thirteen different showdowns between Adam and the Adversary, of which we present a few of the most striking. Since the motif was characteristically repeated with variations (the monkish mind could not resist the temptation to work a good thing to death) it will be necessary to repeat some passages from the book of Moses.
