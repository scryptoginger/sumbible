<Commentary>

The series ends on a piece of luck that Nibley had been complaining about for eleven installments. The Aramaic Enoch fragments from Qumran Cave 4 — the ones he had repeatedly protested were being withheld from the world — were published by J. T. Milik and Matthew Black in 1976, while the series was still appearing. He got them in time for the last article.

**The find.** What he seized on is the name Mahijah. In <VerseRef book="moses" chapter={6} verse="40" /> a man of that name steps out of the crowd and demands that Enoch say plainly who he is and where he comes from — and he is the only non-biblical individual named anywhere in the Enoch material. In the Qumran *Book of Giants*, in fragments Nibley reached at the very end of the volume, a figure called MHWY is repeatedly sent to Enoch by the giants to put questions to him and bring back the answers, flying "like the whirlwind" across the great desert until "he caught sight of Enoch, and he called to him."

Nibley's claim is not merely that the names match, and he is right to insist on the distinction. It is that the *role* matches: in both texts this figure exists for one purpose, to interrogate Enoch and thereby occasion his message. And he notes correctly that the story appears in none of the other Enoch literature — not the Ethiopic, not the Slavonic, not the Greek. It is peculiar to the Aramaic fragments and to Joseph Smith.

**How much weight it bears.** This is the strongest item in the series, and it has become the most cited piece of evidence in Latter-day Saint writing on the Enoch material. Three things should be held alongside it.

The name by itself proves little, and Nibley's own argument shows why. He derives Mahujah and Mahijah from MHWY-EL, the Mehujael of <VerseRef book="genesis" chapter={4} verse="18" /> — which means the name was sitting in the King James Bible, in the Cainite genealogy that <VerseRef book="moses" chapter={5} verse="43" /> was reproducing a few verses earlier. What is not explained by availability is the narrative function, and that is where the interesting question lies.

The fragments are very small. The table Nibley prints is honest about this — the asterisks are lacunae, and the connective tissue between the surviving words is editorial reconstruction. He acknowledges that the pieces are "few and very small and arranged in whatever order the editors think best." The correspondence he draws is real, but it is drawn between a continuous English narrative and a handful of broken Aramaic lines.

And the two figures are not identical. The Qumran Mahaway is a giant, son of the Watcher Baraq'el, who flies to Enoch for the interpretation of dreams; the Mahijah of Moses 6 is a man in a crowd of mortals asking a preacher to identify himself. The shared element — an inquirer named MHWY whose function is to question Enoch — is genuine and unusual. It is not the same scene.

**What Nibley leaves out.** Milik's volume carried a major conclusion that goes unmentioned here: that the Parables of 1 Enoch, the section containing the Son of Man material most attractive to Christian and Latter-day Saint readers, are a late Christian composition rather than a pre-Christian Jewish one. That claim would cut against a good deal of what the earlier installments assumed. In fairness to Nibley, Milik's late dating of the Parables has not aged well and most scholars now place them around the turn of the era — but Nibley did not know that in 1977, and reporting only the parts of a new edition that help is a habit he criticised in others throughout the series.

**Ending.** For all that, the close is genuinely satisfying. A man spends two years arguing that a text he cannot fully test will be vindicated by evidence not yet published, complains bitterly that the evidence is being sat on, and then receives it in time to print it in his final article — and finds in its last pages a name he had flagged as the strangest detail in the whole account. Whatever a reader concludes about the argument, that is a good place for it to stop.

</Commentary>

## The “Enoch” of the Dead Sea Scrolls

Just in time for the latest episode in this examination into the book of Enoch comes the long-awaited translation of the Dead Sea Scroll book of Enoch. (J. T. Milik and M. Black, eds., *The Books of Enoch, Aramaic Fragments of Qumran Cave 4,* Oxford: Clarenden Press, 1976.) Father J. T. Milik, one of the first scholars on the scene when the scrolls were discovered, was assigned thirty-two fragments of the books of Enoch from Qumran Cave IV; and all scholars working on Enoch have eagerly waited during the last quarter century to see what new information would be added, what theories might be toppled, what hypotheses confirmed by these documents in Aramaic, the earliest of all known Enoch texts.

Here’s what they show:

These documents, dating from the third to the first centuries B.C., corroborate the other Enoch literature that we have. There *was* a real book of Enoch, which was once written in five parts. This seriously challenges those critics who have claimed for years that ancient sectaries threw everything into Enoch that they wanted to pass off as scripture.

It’s an added delight for Latter-day Saints to read that Professor Milik finds the Greek texts to be much superior to the Ethiopian texts—the Joseph Smith account in the Pearl of Great Price is closer to the Greek than to the Ethiopian. Latter-day Saints will also note with interest Professor Milik’s deduction that one text, the Gizeh text, was undoubtedly prepared to be buried with the deceased—a parallel with the usage intended for the Abraham text.

Furthermore, Professor Milik works with the fascinating hypothesis that Enoch had prepared an account of the creation and the law of God that naturally predates Moses’ account in Genesis and sees Genesis 6:1–4 [[Gen. 6:1–4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/6?lang=eng&id=p1-p4#p1)], long a puzzling passage to the biblical scholar, as a quotation from that earlier Enoch source. This is *exactly* what happens in the Joseph Smith source: Moses quotes Enoch on events shortly after the creation.

As we have already seen, the Enoch story runs into the oldest literature of the human race; and Professor Milik finds links with the mythological heroes of Sumer and Babylonia, with the astronomy of Egypt and Phoenicia, and the ideas about the earth of Mesopotamia. Even though Professor Milik does not seem to recognize the full importance of the “Enoch figure,” he provides some evidence that undercuts yet another scholarly supposition: that Enoch was invented out of the hopes and yearnings of Messianic Jews in the second century B.C.; in fact, however, these very people were shunning the Enoch material at that very time. Milik reviews some important texts that show the writers of the Aramaic text gradually losing their interest in Enoch material during the first century, then the Essenes turning away from it, the writers at Masada actually expunging the name of Enoch and putting Noah in its stead, while the Christians, on the other hand, treasured it highly and embellished it with so many astrological flourishes that they unintentionally undermined Enoch’s credibility for future generations.

In all of these ways, the Qumran IV Enoch fragments reinforce rather than reinterpret what we as Latter-day Saints already knew about Enoch. But these newly translated pieces add one genuinely new bit of information to our store—something that is probably the most objective test yet of Joseph Smith’s prophetic powers.

What always impressed me as the oddest detail of the Joseph Smith account of Enoch was the appearance out of the blue of the name of the only nonbiblical individual named in the whole book—Mahijah. ([Moses 6:40](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/pgp/moses/6?lang=eng&id=p40#p40).) Mahijah is the one who asks Enoch searching questions, and in answer is told about the place Mahujah, where Enoch began this particular phase of his mission. ([Moses 7:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/pgp/moses/7?lang=eng&id=p2#p2).) It was therefore with a distinct shock of recognition that, after having looked through all but the last of the Aramaic Enoch fragments without finding anything particularly new, and coming to those very last little fragments, I found the name Mahujah leaping out of the pages again and again. (Pp. 300, 302–5, 311, 314.) Could this be our Mahujah or Mahijah? As a matter of fact it could be either, not only because the semi-vowels *w* and *y* are written very much alike in the Aramaic script and are sometimes confused by scribes, but also because the name as written in 4QEn, MHWY, is the same as the MHWY-EL who appears in [Gen. 4:18](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/4?lang=eng&id=p18#p18) as the grandfather of Enoch, transliterated in the King James Bible as *Mehuja-el,* which name also appears in the Greek Septuagint as *Mai-el* and in the Latin Vulgate as *Mavia-el,* showing that Mahujah and Mahijah were the same name.

So what? A coincidence—a giant or a Watcher called Mahujah or Mahijah. But far more than a coincidence when taken in its context. The only thing the Mahijah in the Book of Moses is remarkable for is his putting of bold direct questions to Enoch, thus giving the patriarch an opening for calling upon the people to repent, referring them to the book of remembrance, and telling them of the plan of salvation. And this is exactly the role, and the only role, that the Aramaic Mahujah plays in the story. The name is found in none of the other Enoch texts and neither is the story: it is peculiar to the version Joseph Smith gave us and the oldest known Enoch manuscripts.
