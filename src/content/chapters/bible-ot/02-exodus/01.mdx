Exodus 1 opens the second book of the Pentateuch as a direct continuation of the first. Genesis 50 ended with a coffin in Egypt and Joseph's prophetic charge that "God will surely visit you" (<VerseRef book="genesis" chapter={50} verse="24-25" />); four centuries pass between that promise and the chapter's first verse, and the chapter's job is to set the conditions under which the visiting will need to happen.

The first verses are a roll-call of the seventy who came down with Jacob — Reuben, Simeon, Levi, Judah, Issachar, Zebulun, Benjamin, Dan, Naphtali, Gad, Asher — and a notice that Joseph was already in Egypt. The total: "all the souls that came out of the loins of Jacob were seventy souls." The number is the same one given at <VerseRef book="genesis" chapter={46} verse={27} /> and <VerseRef book="deuteronomy" chapter={10} verse={22} />; the chapter is keeping the count carefully so the next sentence's contrast lands hard. "And Joseph died, and all his brethren, and all that generation. And the children of Israel were fruitful, and increased abundantly, and multiplied, and waxed exceeding mighty; and the land was filled with them."

The vocabulary is the chapter's first theological move. <LangNote term="parah u-ravah" script="פָּרוּ וַיִּשְׁרְצוּ וַיִּרְבּוּ" language="Hebrew" gloss="‘Were fruitful and multiplied’ — the verbs parah (to bear fruit) and ravah (to be many) are the same word-pair God speaks at the creation mandate of Gen 1:28 (‘Be fruitful, and multiply, and replenish the earth’) and the Noahic re-mandate of Gen 9:1. The Israelites' multiplication in Egypt is being framed as the imago-dei creation mandate continuing to operate — the same blessing that filled the earth at creation is now filling Egypt with covenant people. The chapter adds a third verb, sharats (‘to swarm,’ as of sea-creatures in Gen 1:20), which in context is not pejorative but cosmically maximal: the people are multiplying with the abundance of the original creation week.">were fruitful, and increased abundantly, and multiplied</LangNote>. The Israelites are filling Egypt by the same blessing-formula by which God filled the earth at creation. The promise to Abraham of seed "as the stars of heaven for multitude" (<VerseRef book="genesis" chapter={15} verse={5} />) is being fulfilled — quietly, before the new king notices it has happened.

Then 1:8: "Now there arose up a new king over Egypt, which knew not Joseph." The verb <LangNote term="yada" script="יָדַע" language="Hebrew" gloss="‘To know.’ The Hebrew verb is broader than English ‘know’: it covers acquaintance, experience, intimate relationship, and political acknowledgment. The new king's not-knowing Joseph is not just historical ignorance but the rupture of the political memory by which Joseph's family had standing in Egypt. Standard Egyptological commentaries read the verse as marking a dynastic transition — the Hyksos period ending and the New Kingdom (the 18th-19th dynasties) beginning, c. 16th-13th centuries BC. The same verb will return through Exodus: Pharaoh will not ‘know’ the LORD (5:2), and the LORD will eventually make Himself known to both Israel (6:7) and Egypt (7:5, 14:4, 18) through the plagues.">knew not</LangNote> covers political and relational knowing together; the new king has no inherited memory of the Hebrew administrator who preserved Egypt through the famine, and the political accommodation that Joseph's office secured for the family ends. Pharaoh's reasoning is recorded in 1:9-10 as a state-security calculation: the Hebrews are too many, they may join Egypt's enemies in a war, they may flee out of the land. The threat is internal-fifth-column rhetoric; the response will be three escalating policies of suppression.

The first policy is forced labor. Taskmasters are set "over them to afflict them with their burdens. And they built for Pharaoh treasure cities, Pithom and Raamses." The two cities are eastern-delta storage centers; Raamses (per-Ramesses) was a major royal residence in the 19th Egyptian dynasty. The chapter uses the later place-name (<VerseRef book="genesis" chapter={47} verse={11} /> introduced the toponym earlier), presupposing exactly the city the Israelite slaves help build. But the policy fails: "the more they afflicted them, the more they multiplied and grew." The Hebrews' multiplication runs ahead of Pharaoh's repression.

The second policy is a covert order to the Hebrew midwives. Pharaoh calls "the midwives of the Hebrews, of which the name of the one was Shiphrah, and the name of the other Puah" — the chapter records their names with deliberate care, against the chapter's unnamed king. The names mean "beauty / fairness" (<LangNote term="shiphrah" script="שִׁפְרָה" language="Hebrew" gloss="‘Shiphrah’ — ‘beauty, fairness,’ from the verbal root shaphar (to be fair, beautiful). The chapter records the midwives' names with deliberate care against the king of Egypt, who is never named in the entire Exodus narrative. The naming-asymmetry is the chapter's first piece of theological reportage: the empire that thinks itself the world's central power is anonymized; the two foreign midwives are remembered by name.">Shiphrah</LangNote>) and "splendor" or "one who cries out" (<LangNote term="puah" script="פּוּעָה" language="Hebrew" gloss="‘Puah’ — etymology debated; commonly read as ‘splendor’ or, taking the root pu'ah, ‘one who cries out / acts as midwife.’ Like Shiphrah, the name's Hebrew form survives where the king's does not. Two named midwives, one unnamed king — the chapter's quietest theology.">Puah</LangNote>) — though both etymologies are debated. They are likely supervisor-figures over a larger guild of midwives serving the Hebrew population; two persons could not have served a population of hundreds of thousands.

Pharaoh's order is brutal: at every Hebrew birth, "if it be a son, then ye shall kill him: but if it be a daughter, then she shall live." The midwives' response is the chapter's hinge. "But the midwives feared God (<LangNote term="yare-et-elohim" script="יָרֵא אֶת־הָאֱלֹהִים" language="Hebrew" gloss="‘Feared God.’ The Hebrew yare carries both ‘fear’ in the sense of dread and ‘reverence’ in the sense of covenant respect; the construction with elohim names the moral category by which one's loyalties get ranked. The chapter is using the phrase technically: the midwives ranked their loyalty to God above their loyalty to the king, and acted accordingly. Wisdom literature will make the same construction a foundation: ‘The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge’ (Prov 1:7). The chapter's first instance of the phrase in canonical sequence is here, on the lips of women.">feared God</LangNote>), and did not as the king of Egypt commanded them, but saved the men children alive." When Pharaoh questions them, they answer with what may be a deliberate cover-story or may be a true observation about Hebrew births: "the Hebrew women are not as the Egyptian women; for they are lively, and are delivered ere the midwives come in unto them." The chapter records the answer without adjudicating its truthfulness; what the chapter does adjudicate is the divine response: "God dealt well with the midwives: and the people multiplied, and waxed very mighty... and he made them houses." The midwives' covenant fear is rewarded with covenant household.

The third policy is the chapter's darkest. Failing in policy and covertly, Pharaoh commands "all his people, saying, Every son that is born ye shall cast into the river, and every daughter ye shall save alive." The covert order becomes a public state policy of infanticide by river. The river — the Nile, Egypt's life-source — is conscripted as instrument of death against Hebrew infants. The chapter ends without resolution; Pharaoh's last order stands, and the next chapter will open with a Hebrew woman placing her infant son in the Nile in a way that turns the policy back on itself.

For the New Testament, the chapter is summarized at <VerseRef book="acts" chapter={7} verse="17-19" /> in Stephen's speech and at <VerseRef book="hebrews" chapter={11} verse={23} /> in the faith-roll. Hebrews names Moses' parents specifically: "By faith Moses, when he was born, was hid three months of his parents, because they saw he was a proper child; and they were not afraid of the king's commandment." The chapter's drowning-decree is the immediate context for the next chapter's hiding of Moses.

<LangNotes>

**The keyword avad.** Exodus 1's central Hebrew verb is *avad* — to work, serve, labor. Pharaoh makes the Israelites *avad* "with rigor" (1:13-14, *be-pharekh*, "with crushing labor"). The same verb, with the same root, will name the cultic service Israel will eventually render to YHWH at Sinai and after. The Exodus's central question — *whom* will Israel serve? — is set in this chapter's first appearance of the verb. The chapter portrays a wrong *avodah*: forced, brutal, against the family's calling. The covenant *avodah* that follows the deliverance will be a service that is also worship; the same word names both, and the Exodus narrative will be (in part) the substitution of one *avodah* for the other.

**The naming asymmetry.** Exodus 1 names two Hebrew midwives — Shiphrah and Puah — and refuses to name the king of Egypt. Across the entire Exodus narrative, the Pharaoh of the oppression and the Pharaoh of the plagues are never given personal names; the chapter calls only the office. The asymmetry is one of the OT's quietest pieces of theology. The empire that thinks itself the world's center is anonymized; the two foreign midwives who fear God are remembered by name. Pharaonic claims to absolute remembrance — Egyptian royal practice spent vast resources on monumental name-preservation — are silently subverted by a Hebrew text that does not record the king's name and never will.

**The creation-mandate echo.** Exodus 1:7's three-verb pile-up — *paru va-yishretzu va-yirbu* ("were fruitful and swarmed and multiplied") — uses three verbs that all appear in Gen 1's creation accounts: *parah* (be fruitful, Gen 1:22, 28), *sharats* (swarm, Gen 1:20-21), *ravah* (multiply, Gen 1:22, 28). The chapter is framing the Israelite population growth as cosmically maximal — the same blessing that filled the original creation week with living creatures is now filling Egypt with covenant people. The chapter's threat is therefore not just political (a slave revolt) but cosmological (the new king is fighting against the creation blessing itself). The Hebrew Bible's larger argument — that the LORD's covenant blessing cannot be repressed by political power — is set up by this verbal echo on the chapter's eighth verse.

</LangNotes>
