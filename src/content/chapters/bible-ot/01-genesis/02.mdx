Where Genesis 1 paints the cosmos with broad strokes — sun, moon, sea, sky, every kind of life named by category — Genesis 2 narrows abruptly. The wide-angle lens becomes a close one. A single garden. A single man. The intimate, deliberate work of formation: God Himself shaping, breathing, planting, walking. The chapter that follows is less a *second* creation account than a *zoom* — the same God, the same world, the lens now tight.

The man is formed from the dust of the ground, and the Hebrew names the connection. <LangNote term="adam" script="אָדָם" language="Hebrew" gloss="the Hebrew word for 'man' or 'humanity'; it carries the word for ground (adamah) inside it, an audible wordplay throughout the chapter.">The man</LangNote> (*adam*) is formed from the <LangNote term="adamah" script="אֲדָמָה" language="Hebrew" gloss="ground, soil, or earth — the material from which adam is taken; the wordplay with adam is structural to the Genesis 2 narrative.">soil</LangNote> (*adamah*). The very word for humanity carries the word for earth inside it. Into that earth-creature God breathes the <LangNote term="nishmat chayyim" script="נִשְׁמַת חַיִּים" language="Hebrew" gloss="'breath of life' — the divine inbreathing that transforms the dust-formed body into a living being (nephesh chayyah).">breath of life</LangNote>, and "man became a living soul." Body and breath together make him; the dust by itself is just dust.

God then plants a garden in Eden and places the man there "to dress it and to keep it." Two trees stand at the garden's center — the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil — and a river goes out of Eden in four streams, naming a geography that touches real places (Tigris and Euphrates among them) and that has resisted full later identification. Work and worship blur here in a way the Hebrew makes visible: the verbs for "to dress" (*avad*, to serve) and "to keep" (*shamar*, to guard) are the same verbs the book of Numbers later uses for the Levites' service in the tabernacle (<VerseRef book="numbers" chapter={3} verse="7-8" />). The garden is not just a beautiful place; it is sacred space, and the man is its first priest.

God brings the animals to the man to be named — a careful, deliberate scene in which the man is invited into the work of ordering, given the authority to call each kind by its proper name. But among them all there is not found "a help meet for him." No creature on his plane, no partner answerable to the human gift. The lack is the chapter's first explicit *not-good*: "It is not good that the man should be alone."

The Hebrew of what God then makes is worth slowing down for. The phrase translated "help meet" is <LangNote term="ezer kenegdo" script="עֵזֶר כְּנֶגְדּוֹ" language="Hebrew" gloss="'a helper corresponding to him' — though English 'helper' undersells the Hebrew. Ezer (עֵזֶר) occurs 21 times in the Hebrew Bible; the majority refer to God as Israel's helper, including Psalm 33:20 and Deuteronomy 33:7. Kenegdo (כְּנֶגְדּוֹ) means 'corresponding to,' 'opposite,' or 'matching.' The combined sense is a strength facing him, equal in stature, distinct in kind.">*ezer kenegdo*</LangNote> — and *ezer* is not the diminutive English "helper" can suggest. The word is used of God Himself across much of its Old Testament range. The Psalms call God "our help (*ezer*) and our shield" (<VerseRef book="psalms" chapter={33} verse={20} />); Moses, blessing Judah, calls on God as "an help (*ezer*) to him from his enemies" (<VerseRef book="deuteronomy" chapter={33} verse={7} />). To name woman with this word is to name her with the same word the Psalms use for divine strength. *Kenegdo* — "corresponding to him," "opposite him," "matching him" — completes the sense: a strength facing him, equal in stature, distinct in kind.

She is formed from the man's <LangNote term="tsela" script="צֵלָע" language="Hebrew" gloss="'side' or 'rib' — the same Hebrew word later used of the sides or chambers of the tabernacle (Exodus 26:20, 27, 35), suggesting structural foundation rather than merely an isolated bone.">side</LangNote> — the Hebrew used elsewhere for the side or chamber of the tabernacle, suggesting structural foundation rather than a single bone. The man's response is the chapter's only sustained line of poetry: "This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh: she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man." The Hebrew carries the recognition that English can only suggest — <LangNote term="ish / ishah" script="אִישׁ / אִשָּׁה" language="Hebrew" gloss="the words for 'man' and 'woman' share a verbal root; the audible link in 2:23 ('she shall be called Woman [ishah], because she was taken out of Man [ish]') works only in Hebrew.">*ishah* (woman) from *ish* (man)</LangNote>. The chapter ends with the marriage formula the rest of Scripture keeps returning to: "Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife: and they shall be one flesh" — cited as the foundation of marriage by Jesus in <VerseRef book="matthew" chapter={19} verse="4-6" /> and by Paul in <VerseRef book="ephesians" chapter={5} verse={31} />.

The Latter-day Saint canon carries a fuller account of the same events in <VerseRef book="moses" chapter={3} />. Moses 3 frames the Genesis material with an additional doctrinal vocabulary: God says, "I, the Lord God, created all things... spiritually, before they were naturally upon the face of the earth" (<VerseRef book="moses" chapter={3} verse={5} />). The spiritual creation precedes the physical — Adam and Eve, like all things, lived first in heaven before being placed on earth. Read alongside Moses 3, the Genesis 2 narrative takes its place within a larger order: a physical formation following and answering a spiritual one.

<LangNotes>

**The garden as sacred space.** Several details mark Eden as a temple-like setting that the rest of Scripture's sanctuary language will draw on. The verbs *avad* ("to serve") and *shamar* ("to keep / guard"), paired in 2:15 as the man's task in the garden, are the same verbs <VerseRef book="numbers" chapter={3} verse="7-8" /> uses of the Levites' tabernacle service. The cherubim that will guard Eden's entrance in chapter 3 are the same cherubim that flank the ark of the covenant. The river that goes out of Eden to water the world parallels the eschatological river flowing out from the temple in <VerseRef book="ezekiel" chapter={47} /> and from the throne of God in <VerseRef book="revelation" chapter={22} verse="1-2" />. John H. Walton's *The Lost World of Adam and Eve* develops this temple reading at length; older Christian and Jewish commentaries note the same structural parallels. The man is not a gardener whose work happens to be sacred — the garden *is* sacred, and his work in it is priestly from the start.

**Two accounts, one canon.** Critical scholarship since the nineteenth century has often read Genesis 1 and Genesis 2 as originally distinct sources later joined together. The two accounts use different names for God (*Elohim* in chapter 1, *YHWH Elohim* in chapter 2), tell creation in different orders (chapter 1: vegetation, animals, then humans; chapter 2: man, vegetation, animals, then woman), and use different vocabulary. Religious traditions hold this differently. Whatever the compositional history, the canonical text places the two side by side and reads them together: chapter 1 the cosmic frame, chapter 2 the focused interior. The Latter-day Saint reading, in <VerseRef book="moses" chapter={3} />, treats the two as complementary rather than competing — chapter 2 supplies the spiritual-then-physical sequencing that chapter 1 leaves implicit.

</LangNotes>

The English word "helper" has done much of the work of the translation since the King James — and across modern versions it remains the common rendering, with the modifier shifting ("meet," "suitable," "fit," "fitting," "just right"). What the renderings share is the difficulty of capturing *ezer*'s broader Old Testament weight. *Ezer* is the word the Psalms use of God Himself (<VerseRef book="psalms" chapter={121} verse="1-2" />, <VerseRef book="psalms" chapter={33} verse={20} />) — a word for strong rescue, not subordinate service. The English "helper" risks domesticating it. *Kenegdo* — "corresponding to him" or "opposite him" — completes the phrase by naming her as a counterpart, equal and answering. The phrase, taken whole in the Hebrew, names a partnership of strengths.
