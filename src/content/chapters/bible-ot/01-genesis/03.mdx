Genesis 3 is the pivot of primeval history. Everything that comes before — the spoken-into-order cosmos, the planted garden, the formed humanity, the gift of marriage — is the world as God made it. Everything that comes after will be the world living with what is undone here. The chapter has only twenty-four verses, but it sets in place the entire later vocabulary of sin and grace, death and promise, exile and return.

The chapter opens with a wordplay only the Hebrew can carry. Chapter 2 closed with the man and woman <LangNote term="arom" script="עָרוֹם" language="Hebrew" gloss="naked. The Hebrew shares its three consonants with arum (עָרוּם), 'subtle' or 'crafty,' set right at the chapter break — a literary hinge.">naked</LangNote> and unashamed; chapter 3 opens with the <LangNote term="nachash" script="נָחָשׁ" language="Hebrew" gloss="serpent. The chapter introduces it simply as one of the beasts of the field; Revelation 12:9 names it the devil and Satan, and the Pearl of Great Price (Moses 4:5-6) tells the inside of the story.">serpent</LangNote> as <LangNote term="arum" script="עָרוּם" language="Hebrew" gloss="subtle, crafty, shrewd. The Hebrew sets arum (3:1) and arom (2:25) right against each other across the chapter break — the audible pun underscores the contrast between the human pair's innocence and the creature about to exploit it.">more subtle</LangNote> than any beast of the field — *arum* against *arom*, the same consonants pulled in opposite directions. The human pair stand exposed; the serpent moves with concealed motive.

The temptation works by reframing. God is presented as withholding rather than providing; the prohibition becomes a slight rather than a kindness; the unknown ("ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil") becomes more desirable than the known. The woman sees the fruit as good for food, pleasant to the eyes, desirable to make one wise — three notes that the New Testament will echo as the pattern of all worldly temptation (<VerseRef book="1-john" chapter={2} verse={16} />). She eats; she gives; he eats. The text is sparing. The act itself is one verse.

The aftermath unfolds in cadence. Their eyes are opened — and the first thing they see is their own nakedness. They sew fig leaves; they hide. God walks in the garden "in the cool of the day," and they hide further. Then the divine confrontation, three short questions: "Where art thou? Who told thee that thou wast naked? Hast thou eaten of the tree?" Each pushes inward; each is met with deflection. The man blames the woman ("the woman whom thou gavest to be with me"); the woman blames the serpent. The chain of blame reaches its end in the serpent, who is given no defense.

The curses follow, structured in three: serpent, woman, man. The Hebrew of the woman's and the man's curses share a single word — <LangNote term="eitsavon" script="עִצָּבוֹן" language="Hebrew" gloss="sorrow, painful toil. The same word appears in both the woman's curse (3:16, in childbearing) and the man's (3:17, in working the ground), binding the two together.">*eitsavon*</LangNote>, "painful toil" — binding her labor in childbearing and his labor in the field as parallel forms of the same fallen condition. The ground that gave forth its fruit freely now resists. The body that was made to bring forth life will do so in pain. Death enters: "dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return."

But woven into the curse on the serpent is the first promise. "I will put enmity between thee and the woman, and between thy seed and her seed; it shall bruise thy head, and thou shalt bruise his heel." The Christian tradition has called this verse the **protoevangelium** — the first gospel — since Irenaeus in the second century. The Hebrew <LangNote term="zera" script="זֶרַע" language="Hebrew" gloss="seed, offspring. A collective singular: it can refer to a whole lineage or to one individual descendant. The Septuagint translates with the masculine singular pronoun autos (he), where the neuter would be grammatically expected — signaling that ancient translators already read the verse as pointing to a particular descendant.">*zera*</LangNote> ("seed") is grammatically a collective singular, capable of referring to a whole lineage or to one individual descendant; the Greek Septuagint, translating into a language where the noun *sperma* takes a neuter pronoun, departs from grammatical agreement to use the masculine *autos* — a small grammatical choice that signals an interpretation already in motion: a particular descendant, a coming Deliverer, who will at last crush the serpent's head.

The chapter ends with two small acts that show grace and judgment together. God Himself makes "coats of skins" and clothes them — the first sacrifice in Scripture, an animal dying to cover human shame. And He drives them out of the garden, stationing cherubim with a flaming sword "to keep the way of the tree of life." The way is closed, but it is not unmarked. The tree of life will be the last image of the canonical Bible (<VerseRef book="revelation" chapter={22} verse={2} />), restored at the end.

The Latter-day Saint canon brings the chapter into a fuller frame. <VerseRef book="moses" chapter={4} /> records the same events with the inside revealed: Satan is named explicitly as the one acting through the serpent; the rebellion in heaven, in which Satan sought to redeem all humanity at the cost of agency, is set as the prelude. And in <VerseRef book="2-nephi" chapter={2} verse="22-25" />, Lehi gives the doctrine its most concentrated expression: "If Adam had not transgressed he would not have fallen... Adam fell that men might be; and men are, that they might have joy." Read alongside <VerseRef book="moses" chapter={4} /> and <VerseRef book="2-nephi" chapter={2} />, the Fall is not only loss but the necessary opening of the door — to mortality, to agency, to the very possibility of the joy Christ's Atonement secures.

<LangNotes>

**The "fortunate fall" in Latter-day Saint reading.** The Christian tradition has long held the Fall and the Incarnation together — Augustine and the medieval *felix culpa* tradition spoke of the "happy fault" that occasioned the coming of so great a Redeemer. The Latter-day Saint reading goes further, treating the Fall as not merely permitted-and-redeemed but as necessary from the start — a step within God's plan rather than a derailment of it. Lehi's discourse in <VerseRef book="2-nephi" chapter={2} verse="22-25" /> is the foundational text: without the Fall, no children, no agency, no growth, no joy. <VerseRef book="sections" chapter={29} verse={39} /> states the principle in another form: "it must needs be that the devil should tempt the children of men, or they could not be agents unto themselves." The Fall remains tragedy — death and pain and exile are real — but it is tragedy within providence. The chapter that introduces sin also opens the door to the Atonement that will answer it.

**The cherubim and the tree of life.** The cherubim stationed at the east of Eden (3:24) recur throughout Scripture's sanctuary imagery: cherubim cover the mercy seat above the ark (<VerseRef book="exodus" chapter={25} verse="18-22" />); their forms are woven into the tabernacle and temple veils (<VerseRef book="exodus" chapter={26} verse={31} />); they appear in Ezekiel's throne vision (<VerseRef book="ezekiel" chapter={1} />) and again in <VerseRef book="ezekiel" chapter={10} />. Eden was sacred space (see the chapter 2 notes); the cherubim that guard the way back to it are the same figures that mark the holy places of Israel's worship. The way to the tree of life is closed; the cherubim mark that it remains, and that one day — at <VerseRef book="revelation" chapter={22} verse={2} /> — it will be opened again.

</LangNotes>

A single pronoun, four different choices — and they bear a long interpretive history. The Hebrew *zera* ("seed") is grammatically a collective singular, so the pronoun that follows can be read as referring to either an individual ("he") or the whole lineage ("they"); the verb agreement allows either. The Greek Septuagint chose *autos* (he, masculine singular), departing from the grammatically expected neuter — a translation choice that already reads the verse as pointing to a particular descendant. The Vulgate's "she" (often noted in Catholic interpretation in connection with Marian theology) reflects a Latin textual tradition; modern Catholic translations (NABRE, RNJB) align with "he." The JPS reads "they," capturing the collective sense. The Christian and Latter-day Saint traditions both read the verse as the protoevangelium — the first promise of a coming Deliverer — with the messianic reading dominant in the patristic, medieval, Reformation, and Restoration receptions of the passage alike.
</TranslationCompare>
