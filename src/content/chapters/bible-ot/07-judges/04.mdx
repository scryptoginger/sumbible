Judges 4 narrates the Deborah-Barak deliverance from Jabin king of Canaan — the cycle's most narratively-extended pre-Gideon execution and the first major-judge cycle led by a prophetess. Four movements: the cycle's reopening (4:1-3); Deborah's summons of Barak and the prophecy of Sisera's fall into a woman's hand (4:4-10); the battle at Kishon (4:11-16); Sisera's flight to Jael's tent and the prophecy's literal fulfillment (4:17-24).

**The cycle reopens** (4:1-3). The chapter opens at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={4} verse={1} />: "And the children of Israel again did evil in the sight of the LORD, when Ehud was dead." The cyclic-pattern installed at Judges 2:11-23 (SB1 prior block) executes in compressed-clean form once more: did-evil → sold-into-hand-of-oppressor. The oppressor at this cycle is Jabin king of Canaan reigning at Hazor; his captain Sisera dwells at Harosheth-of-the-Gentiles; the oppression includes "nine hundred chariots of iron" (4:3) — the iron-chariots framework's most-developed instance (see LangNote below). The duration is twenty years.

**Deborah's summons of Barak + the prophecy of Sisera's fall into a woman's hand** (4:4-10). The chapter introduces Deborah at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={4} verse="4-5" />: "And Deborah, a prophetess, the wife of Lapidoth, she judged Israel at that time. And she dwelt under the palm tree of Deborah between Ramah and Bethel in mount Ephraim: and the children of Israel came up to her for judgment." Deborah is the first figure in the canonical narrative to hold the combined offices of prophetess and judge — the *nevi'ah* and *shofet* roles together. Her judicial seat is named ("the palm tree of Deborah"), and the people come to her for judgment, indicating an established judicial-ministry that precedes the military-deliverance the chapter goes on to narrate.

The summons of Barak comes at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={4} verse="6-7" />: "Hath not the LORD God of Israel commanded, saying, Go and draw toward mount Tabor, and take with thee ten thousand men of the children of Naphtali and of the children of Zebulun? And I will draw unto thee to the river Kishon Sisera, the captain of Jabin's army, with his chariots and his multitude; and I will deliver him into thine hand." Barak's response at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={4} verse={8} /> is conditional: "If thou wilt go with me, then I will go: but if thou wilt not go with me, then I will not go." Deborah agrees but issues the prophecy that frames the rest of the chapter at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={4} verse={9} />: "I will surely go with thee: notwithstanding the journey that thou takest shall not be for thine honour; for the LORD shall sell Sisera into the hand of a woman." The prophecy's fulfillment will come not through Deborah herself (she does not strike Sisera) but through Jael at the chapter's end.

**The battle at Kishon** (4:11-16). The chapter installs a brief geographic-and-genealogical note at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={4} verse={11} /> — Heber the Kenite (of the line of Hobab, Moses' father-in-law) had pitched his tent at the plain of Zaanaim, near Kedesh — which sets up the Jael scene by establishing the Kenite-Canaanite peace-relationship (4:17) that motivates Sisera's flight to Jael's tent.

The battle itself executes Deborah's command at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={4} verse={14} />: "Up; for this is the day in which the LORD hath delivered Sisera into thine hand: is not the LORD gone out before thee?" The deliverance is theologically attributed: "And the LORD discomfited Sisera, and all his chariots, and all his host, with the edge of the sword before Barak; so that Sisera lighted down off his chariot, and fled away on his feet" (4:15). The iron-chariots framework that had bounded the actual-occupation register (see LangNote below) is here narratively-resolved: the chariots were never the LORD's obstacle.

**Sisera's flight to Jael's tent + the prophecy's literal fulfillment** (4:17-24). Sisera flees on foot to the tent of Jael wife of Heber the Kenite — because of the peace-relationship between Jabin's house and Heber's house. Jael's words at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={4} verse={18} /> are deliberate-hospitality-framing: "Turn in, my lord, turn in to me; fear not." She covers him with a mantle, gives him milk when he asks for water (4:19), and accepts his instruction to stand at the door and turn away inquirers (4:20). Then at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={4} verse={21} />: "Then Jael Heber's wife took a nail of the tent, and took an hammer in her hand, and went softly unto him, and smote the nail into his temples, and fastened it into the ground: for he was fast asleep and weary. So he died."

The chapter's framing of the act sits in a multi-position interpretive spectrum (see LangNote below) — SumBible reports the spectrum without arbitration per §0 Prime Directive #5. The canonical narrative's own text-internal framing operates at two registers simultaneously: the prophecy-fulfillment frame at 4:9 ("the LORD shall sell Sisera into the hand of a woman") and the theological-attribution frame at 4:23 ("So God subdued on that day Jabin the king of Canaan before the children of Israel"). The Song of Deborah at Judges 5 (next chapter, SB4) will give the canonical-Hebrew-poetic celebration register of the same event.

Barak's arrival closes the scene at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={4} verse={22} />: "And, behold, as Barak pursued Sisera, Jael came out to meet him, and said unto him, Come, and I will shew thee the man whom thou seekest. And when he came into her tent, behold, Sisera lay dead, and the nail was in his temples." The chapter closes (4:23-24) with the historical-political summary: Jabin is subdued, then progressively destroyed by Israel.

**The Jael-Sisera spectrum reported without arbitration.** The Jael narrative at 4:17-22 sits in a multi-position interpretive spectrum across the Christian-and-Jewish reception-history. SumBible reports the spectrum per §0 Prime Directive #5 (when uncertain, omit arbitration) and §6.0 (spectrum-reporting on contested interpretations). Four major position-types operate:

(1) **Heroic-deliverance reading.** Jael is the exemplary instrument of the LORD's covenantal-deliverance. Her deed is celebrated in canonical Hebrew poetry at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={5} verse={24} /> (next chapter): "Blessed above women shall Jael the wife of Heber the Kenite be, blessed shall she be above women in the tent." The chapter at hand's framing presents the act as the prophecy-fulfillment promised at 4:9 — "the LORD shall sell Sisera into the hand of a woman" — and the theological-attribution at 4:23 reads the entire deliverance (including Jael's act) as God's-action. On this reading, the canonical narrative's text-internal framing endorses Jael's deed as righteous-deliverance.

(2) **Morally-problematic-deception reading.** The Ancient Near Eastern hospitality-framework treats giving shelter and food as an inviolable sacred-bond — the framework Gen 18-19 (Abraham hosting the three visitors, Lot hosting them at Sodom) installs canonically. Jael's offer of milk when Sisera asked for water, her covering of him with a mantle, her assurance ("fear not"), and her acceptance of his instructions are all hospitality-acts in standard ANE register. The subsequent killing while he sleeps is therefore a violation of the hospitality-framework. Some commentary positions read this as a moral-cost the narrative records without endorsing — the narrative's prophecy-fulfillment framing operates at the deliverance-outcome register, not at the methodology-moral-evaluation register.

(3) **ANE-hospitality-violation-as-narrative-significance reading.** The hospitality-framework's violation is itself what makes the act narratively-significant. The inversion (a Canaanite-allied Kenite woman violating the hospitality her tribe has historically extended to the Canaanite-coalition) marks Sisera's complete defeat under conditions even an oppressor would have considered the safest possible. On this reading, the canonical narrative's framing operates as historical-political-record at the level of register; the moral-evaluation question is bracketed as not the narrative's primary concern.

(4) **Sacred-narrative-recording-without-endorsing reading.** Parallel to the Ehud spectrum at Judg 3 (drafted SB2 prior block): the canonical canvas records the historical-and-political reality of the deliverance without underwriting every operational detail as morally exemplary. The Hebrews 11:32 NT-reception's selection of Barak (not Jael) for the faith-paradigm cloud-of-witnesses register has been read by some commentary as the NT's implicit register-positioning — Barak's faith is the celebrated-faith; Jael's methodology is registered without celebration at the NT-register.

The canonical narrative's own framing data: the prophecy-frame at 4:9 (Deborah's prophecy of Sisera's fall into a woman's hand); the theological-attribution at 4:23 (God subdued Jabin); the poetic-celebration at Judg 5:24-27 (next chapter; the most explicit celebration register). The Song of Deborah's celebration of Jael at 5:24-27 is the canonical narrative's strongest celebratory register; the chapter at hand's prose register is more reserved-narrative-recording with the prophecy-fulfillment frame doing the interpretive work. SumBible reports the spectrum, identifies the canonical text-internal framing data, and does not advocate a single reading as canonical-correct.

**The Heb 11:32 cluster — Barak's faith-paradigm inclusion.** Hebrews 11:32 names four Judges-figures in the faith-paradigm cloud-of-witnesses: "And what shall I more say? for the time would fail me to tell of Gedeon, and of Barak, and of Samson, and of Jephthae; of David also, and Samuel, and of the prophets." The Hebrews-author's selection of these four registers Barak's faith — specifically, the faith that brought him to mount Tabor with ten thousand men at Deborah's word despite the 900 iron chariots arrayed against him. Barak's request at Judg 4:8 ("If thou wilt go with me, then I will go") is read by some commentary positions as moral-failure-of-courage (Deborah's 4:9 response, "the journey that thou takest shall not be for thine honour," is read as rebuke); but the Hebrews 11:32 selection of Barak for the faith-paradigm cloud-of-witnesses reads him at the faith-register — the act of going at Deborah's word against the iron-chariots-armed-coalition is the faith-act the NT reception celebrates. The register-distinction is theologically substantive: the same act registers differently when read at the moral-evaluation register vs the faith-register, and the NT explicitly elects the faith-register. The cloud-of-witnesses' general description at Heb 11:33-34 ("Who through faith subdued kingdoms, wrought righteousness, obtained promises, stopped the mouths of lions. Quenched the violence of fire, escaped the edge of the sword, out of weakness were made strong, waxed valiant in fight, turned to flight the armies of the aliens") covers Barak's narrative-fit at multiple points — particularly "out of weakness were made strong" and "turned to flight the armies of the aliens." The remaining three Judges-figures (Gedeon, Samson, Jephthae) will be referenced in their respective later-session chapters; the four-name cluster here is the cross-canon connection-back the Judges-book opens.

**The iron-chariots framework's cross-session narrative-resolution at Kishon.** The iron-chariots framework's narrative-arc is one of the cross-session frameworks consummated in this chapter. Three prior installations frame the chapter's resolution: Joshua 17:16-18 (Sub-Batch 2 Session 23) installed the framework with the children of Joseph's protest and Joshua's promise that "thou shalt drive out the Canaanites, though they have iron chariots, and though they be strong"; Judges 1:19 (SB1 this session) marked the framework's actual-occupation tension ("the LORD was with Judah; and he drave out the inhabitants of the mountain; but could not drive out the inhabitants of the valley, because they had chariots of iron"); the chapter at hand at 4:3 + 4:13 brings the framework to its most-developed instance with Sisera's nine hundred chariots of iron. The narrative-resolution comes at 4:15 — the LORD discomfits Sisera and his chariots, and Sisera himself "lighted down off his chariot, and fled away on his feet." The iron-chariots were never the LORD's obstacle; the prior territorial-incomplete tension was Israel's incomplete response, not divine-incapacity. The Song of Deborah (Judges 5 next chapter, SB4 extra-care) will give the poetic-celebration register of the same Kishon-resolution at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={5} verse={21} />: "the river of Kishon swept them away."
