Judges 10 transitions between the Abimelech narrative (Judg 9, Session 26) and the Jephthah cycle (Judg 11, next chapter). Four movements compose the chapter: Tola the Issacharite (10:1-2); Jair the Gileadite (10:3-5); the cycle's seven-gods apostasy-onset and double-oppression by Philistines and Ammon (10:6-9); the LORD's "I will deliver you no more" scene and Israel's persistence (10:10-16); the Gilead-leadership question that sets up Jephthah's call (10:17-18).

**Tola the Issacharite** (10:1-2). The chapter opens at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={10} verse={1} />: "And after Abimelech there arose to defend Israel Tola the son of Puah, the son of Dodo, a man of Issachar; and he dwelt in Shamir in mount Ephraim." The "after Abimelech" notation places Tola's judgeship as the cycle's stabilization-response to the chaos Judg 9 closed. Two structural notes: Tola is from Issachar (the first canonical-named Issacharite judge) but resides in mount Ephraim (cross-tribal placement); he judges Israel twenty-three years; he dies and is buried in Shamir. The form-as-substance pattern established at Shamgar (Judg 3:31, Session 24 SB2) — minor-judge notations of one to five verses without oppressor-naming or cycle-completion narrative — recurs here.

**Jair the Gileadite** (10:3-5). The Jair notation at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={10} verse="3-4" /> adds a distinctive triple-symmetry: twenty-two years of judgeship, thirty sons riding thirty ass colts in thirty cities called Havoth-jair "in the land of Gilead." The thirty/thirty/thirty enumeration is the form's most-developed single-figure instance; the geographic anchor in trans-Jordan Gilead prepares the Ammonite-oppression theater that follows. Tola's twenty-three + Jair's twenty-two together yield forty-five years of stable judgeship between Abimelech and the Ammonite cycle.

**The seven-gods apostasy + double oppression** (10:6-9). The cycle's apostasy-onset comes at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={10} verse={6} />: "And the children of Israel did evil again in the sight of the LORD, and served Baalim, and Ashtaroth, and the gods of Syria, and the gods of Zidon, and the gods of Moab, and the gods of the children of Ammon, and the gods of the Philistines, and forsook the LORD, and served not him." The seven-gods list is the longest single foreign-gods enumeration in Judges to this point — the cyclic-pattern's deteriorative trajectory registered at the apostasy-extent level (see LangNote below). The LORD's response at 10:7 is a double-oppression: Israel is sold into the hands of the Philistines and the children of Ammon simultaneously — the only such double-oppression notation in the book. The Ammonite oppression's specific scope at 10:8-9 is eighteen years in trans-Jordan Israel + Ammon crossing Jordan to fight against Judah, Benjamin, and the house of Ephraim. Israel is "sore distressed."

**The LORD's "I will deliver you no more" scene** (10:10-16). The chapter's structural climax is the cyclic-pattern's only instance where the LORD initially refuses to deliver. Israel cries at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={10} verse={10} /> with explicit confession: "We have sinned against thee, both because we have forsaken our God, and also served Baalim." The LORD's response at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={10} verse="11-13" /> recounts seven prior deliverances — Egyptians, Amorites, Ammon, Philistines, Zidonians, Amalekites, Maonites — answering the seven-gods apostasy of 10:6 with a seven-deliverances inventory; the symmetry between the two sevens is structural-deliberate. Then comes the refusal: "Yet ye have forsaken me, and served other gods: wherefore I will deliver you no more. Go and cry unto the gods which ye have chosen; let them deliver you in the time of your tribulation" (10:13-14). The redirection-to-the-chosen-gods operates at the same register as the Bochim angel-of-the-LORD's covenantal-lawsuit at Judg 2:3 (Session 24 SB1): the consequence of choosing other gods is being directed to them at the moment of need.

Israel's response at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={10} verse={15} /> is persistence of confession: "We have sinned: do thou unto us whatsoever seemeth good unto thee; deliver us only, we pray thee, this day." The second clause — *do thou unto us whatsoever seemeth good* — submits to the LORD's judgment unconditionally; the third clause — *deliver us only* — asks for deliverance without earning it. Then Israel acts at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={10} verse={16} />: "And they put away the strange gods from among them, and served the LORD." The cycle's apostasy-stage is reversed not by request but by reformation. The LORD's response is the chapter's most-discussed single phrase: "and his soul was grieved for the misery of Israel."

**The Gilead-leadership question** (10:17-18). The chapter closes with Ammon encamped in Gilead and Israel encamped at Mizpeh; the princes of Gilead ask at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={10} verse={18} />: "What man is he that will begin to fight against the children of Ammon? he shall be head over all the inhabitants of Gilead." The leadership question's open-ended formulation — *what man is he* — sets up the Jephthah-call narrative at Judges 11. Jephthah is not named here; the chapter's structural function is to install the mechanism by which Judges 11 will narrate his recruitment from exile.

**The cyclic-pattern's deteriorative trajectory at its deepest instance to this point — the seven-gods + seven-deliverances symmetry + the LORD's reluctance scene.** Three structural features compose the chapter's distinctive contribution to the cyclic-pattern framework installed at Judg 2:11-23 (Session 24 SB1):

(1) **The seven-gods apostasy-list at 10:6 as the framework's most-developed instance to this point.** Prior apostasy-onset notations in Judges have named one or two foreign gods (3:7's "Baalim and the groves"; 6:1's general "did evil"; 8:33's "Baalberith"). The 10:6 enumeration names seven: Baalim, Ashtaroth, and the gods of Syria, Zidon, Moab, Ammon, and Philistines. The enumeration's symmetry — three pairs (Baalim/Ashtaroth, Syria/Zidon, Moab/Ammon) plus the singular Philistines as seventh — and its geographic comprehensiveness (north, east, southeast, west) register Israel's apostasy as comprehensively-syncretistic. The Judg 2:19 framework-statement ("they returned, and corrupted themselves more than their fathers" — drafted Session 24 SB1) operates here at the apostasy-extent level: each cycle's apostasy exceeds the prior cycle's, and 10:6 is the framework's deepest instance through Judg 10.

(2) **The seven-deliverances answer at 10:11-12.** The LORD's recap of prior deliverances names seven: Egyptians, Amorites, children of Ammon, Philistines, Zidonians, Amalekites, Maonites. The symmetry between the seven-gods apostasy of 10:6 and the seven-deliverances inventory of 10:11-12 is structural-deliberate. The chapter at hand registers a covenantal-symmetry: comprehensive apostasy (seven foreign gods) met with comprehensive faithfulness-recap (seven prior deliverances). The covenantal-evaluation framework operates at the same register as Joshua 24:2-13's first-person-divine historical-recap (drafted Session 23 SB6) — both passages list the LORD's redemptive-acts as the ground for the covenantal-demand that follows. The Judg 10 instance is the cyclic-pattern's distinctive seven/seven elaboration.

(3) **The 10:13-16 reluctance-scene as the cycle's structural inflection point.** This is the only instance in the book of Judges where the LORD initially refuses to deliver. The refusal is grounded in the cycle's prior pattern — Israel has been delivered seven times and returned to apostasy each time; the LORD's "I will deliver you no more" registers the framework's deteriorative-trajectory as approaching its limit. The redirection at 10:14 ("Go and cry unto the gods which ye have chosen; let them deliver you in the time of your tribulation") operates as the framework's negative-execution at the rhetorical register — the LORD names the apostasy's consequence as being left to the chosen gods at the moment of need. Israel's response is what changes the trajectory: not request, but reformation — Israel puts away the strange gods and serves the LORD (10:16a). The chapter at hand registers idol-disposal as the framework's reversal-mechanism; the cyclic-pattern's apostasy-stage is reversible not by confession alone but by confession-plus-action.

(4) **The 10:16b "his soul was grieved for the misery of Israel" anthropomorphic-affect notation.** The Hebrew *vatiqtsar nafsho ba'amal yisrael* ("and his soul was shortened / impatient at the labor of Israel") is one of the OT's strongest anthropomorphic affect-attributions to the LORD — the LORD's-own-*nefesh* (soul) responding emotionally to Israel's distress. The construction parallels Gen 6:6 ("it repented the LORD that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him at his heart") at the affect-attribution register, though Gen 6:6 operates at the pre-flood judgment register and 10:16 at the post-confession-deliverance register. The believer-spectrum reads the anthropomorphic-affect construction across multiple positions — literal divine-emotional-response / accommodated-divine-self-disclosure / phenomenological-narrative-language — none of which the chapter at hand arbitrates. SumBible reports the construction's force without resolving the philosophical-theological question; the canonical narrative carries the affect-language as theologically substantive without specifying its metaphysical mechanism.

The Jephthah-call mechanism set up at 10:17-18 ("What man is he that will begin to fight... he shall be head over all the inhabitants of Gilead") closes the chapter by handing off to Judg 11. Two structural notations: the Gilead leadership-vacancy is treated as solvable by acclamation (whoever leads the fight will lead the people); the leadership-acclamation framework operates outside the LORD-raises-up-a-judge pattern that has governed the earlier cycles. The Jephthah cycle will install the deliverer through a process that mixes elder-recruitment, vow, and the Spirit-of-the-LORD coming upon Jephthah at 11:29 — a hybrid framework that registers the cyclic-pattern's continued deteriorative complexity into the chapter that follows.
