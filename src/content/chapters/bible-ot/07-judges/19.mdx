Judges 19 opens the second appendix narrative's descent from cultic disorder to communal violence. The same interpretive formula that governed Micah's shrine and the Danite migration returns at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={19} verse={1} />: "there was no king in Israel." The phrase does not make monarchy a simple cure for sin; it frames the episode as a world where covenant order, hospitality, protection for the vulnerable, and tribal justice have collapsed into what the closing formula of <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={17} verse={6} /> already named — every man doing what is right in his own eyes.

**The Levite, the concubine, and the delayed departure** (19:1-10). The chapter begins with a Levite sojourning "on the side of mount Ephraim" who takes a concubine from Bethlehem-judah. She leaves for her father's house, and the Levite follows "to speak friendly unto her" (19:3). The father-in-law's repeated hospitality delays departure through meal after meal and night after night. The delay is narratively important: by the time the Levite finally leaves, daylight is almost gone, forcing the later decision about where to lodge. The chapter's domestic opening is therefore not detached from the public disaster that follows; household choices, hospitality customs, and covenant-community obligations all converge on the road.

**Jebus avoided, Gibeah chosen** (19:11-15). Near Jebus, the servant proposes stopping, but the Levite refuses to lodge in a city of strangers and presses on toward an Israelite city, Gibeah or Ramah. The tragic irony is severe: the non-Israelite city is avoided as unsafe, while Benjaminite Gibeah becomes the place of outrage. When the travelers sit in the open place of the city, "there was no man that took them into his house to lodging" (19:15). Ancient hospitality is not a decorative custom here; it is the first moral test Gibeah fails. Only an old man, himself from mount Ephraim and sojourning in Gibeah, receives them.

**Gibeah as Israel's Sodom-pattern** (19:16-26). The old host's welcome is immediately threatened when "certain sons of Belial" surround the house and demand the male guest. The scene deliberately recalls <VerseRef book="genesis" chapter={19} verse="1-11" />: strangers in the open place, a host's urgent reception, the house surrounded, and a demand that exposes a city's corruption. Judges intensifies the horror by setting that pattern inside Israel. The host's counteroffer of women, and the Levite's surrender of his concubine, are not models of righteousness. The narrative presents a world in which male honor, guest-protection, and self-preservation are defended at the cost of a woman's life. The woman is abused through the night and falls at the door of the house, her hands on the threshold. The threshold detail is devastating: she reaches the boundary of shelter, but no shelter is finally given.

**The morning and the summons to Israel** (19:27-30). The Levite's morning words, "Up, and let us be going," are chillingly inadequate to what has happened. When she does not answer, he carries her home, divides her body into twelve pieces, and sends them through the coasts of Israel. The act is shocking, but it is also a summons: the tribes must see what has been done and decide whether covenant order still matters. The final words of the chapter — "Consider of it, take advice, and speak your minds" — turn the outrage into a national judicial crisis. Judges 20 will show Israel answering that summons, but Judges 19 ends before judgment is rendered, leaving the reader with the evidence and the demand for deliberation.

<LangNotes>

**"Sons of Belial" and the chapter's moral vocabulary.** The KJV phrase "sons of Belial" at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={19} verse={22} /> translates a Hebrew idiom often understood in context as men of worthlessness, vileness, or destructive lawlessness. Later Jewish and Christian usage can treat Belial as a more developed proper name for evil, but in Judges the phrase functions primarily as moral characterization: these are men whose conduct rejects covenant restraint. The expression prepares for the legal language of <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={20} verse={13} />, where Israel demands that Benjamin deliver up "the men, the children of Belial, which are in Gibeah" so that evil may be put away from Israel.

**The twelve-piece summons and Saul's later symbolic act.** <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={19} verse={29} /> sends the divided body "into all the coasts of Israel." Commentators often compare <VerseRef book="1-samuel" chapter={11} verse={7} />, where Saul later sends pieces of oxen through Israel to summon the tribes against Ammon. The comparison is formal rather than moral equivalence: Saul's sign threatens the negligent; the Levite's sign forces the nation to look at a crime that would otherwise remain local. In Judges, the symbol exposes how far the covenant people have fallen and sets up the near-destruction of Benjamin in chapters 20-21.

</LangNotes>
