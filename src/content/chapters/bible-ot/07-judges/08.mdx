Judges 8 closes the Gideon cycle across nine movements that together register the cycle's deteriorative trajectory: Ephraim's complaint and the diplomatic response (8:1-3); pursuit of Zebah and Zalmunna and Succoth/Penuel's refusal (8:4-9); the kings' capture (8:10-12); accountability against Succoth and Penuel (8:13-17); the kings' execution (8:18-21); the anti-monarchy declaration (8:22-23); the ephod compromise (8:24-27); rest and Gideon's death (8:28-32); the cyclic-apostasy-onset (8:33-35).

**Ephraim's complaint + Gideon's diplomatic response** (8:1-3). Ephraim's complaint at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={8} verse={1} /> ("Why hast thou served us thus, that thou calledst us not, when thou wentest to fight with the Midianites?") raises an inter-tribal tension the Judg 5 Song of Deborah's tribal-participation roll-call (Session 24 SB4) had already registered as a recurring issue. Gideon's response at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={8} verse="2-3" /> deflects the complaint by exalting Ephraim's actual contribution: "Is not the gleaning of the grapes of Ephraim better than the vintage of Abiezer? God hath delivered into your hands the princes of Midian, Oreb and Zeeb: and what was I able to do in comparison of you?" The gleaning-vs-vintage formulation — the gleanings (the leftover gathering) of Ephraim better than the full harvest of Abiezer (Gideon's clan) — is a rhetorical-humility move that dissolves the tension; "their anger was abated toward him, when he had said that" (8:3).

**Pursuit + Succoth and Penuel's refusal** (8:4-9). Gideon and his 300 cross Jordan in pursuit of Zebah and Zalmunna — "faint, yet pursuing them" (8:4). The request to Succoth for bread is refused at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={8} verse={6} />: "Are the hands of Zebah and Zalmunna now in thine hand, that we should give bread unto thine army?" Gideon's reply at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={8} verse={7} /> threatens future judgment: "Therefore when the LORD hath delivered Zebah and Zalmunna into mine hand, then I will tear your flesh with the thorns of the wilderness and with briers." Penuel responds the same way; Gideon's threat at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={8} verse={9} /> is brief: "When I come again in peace, I will break down this tower."

**The kings' capture** (8:10-12). At Karkor Zebah and Zalmunna have 15,000 men with them — all that remained "of all the hosts of the children of the east: for there fell an hundred and twenty thousand men that drew sword" (8:10). The arithmetic registers the scale: 135,000 total at the start of the campaign; 120,000 already slain in the Judg 7 trumpets-and-torches victory and pursuit; 15,000 remaining. Gideon goes by the way of the tent-dwellers and "smote the host; for the host was secure" (8:11); the kings flee but are captured (8:12).

**Accountability against Succoth and Penuel** (8:13-17). Gideon's return from battle "before the sun was up" (8:13) signals the accountability sequence. A young man of Succoth describes the city's 77 elders to Gideon by name (8:14); Gideon confronts the men of Succoth at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={8} verse={15} />: "Behold Zebah and Zalmunna, with whom ye did upbraid me, saying, Are the hands of Zebah and Zalmunna now in thine hand, that we should give bread unto thy men that are weary?" The execution of the threat follows at 8:16: "And he took the elders of the city, and thorns of the wilderness and briers, and with them he taught the men of Succoth." The Penuel judgment at 8:17 is briefer: "And he beat down the tower of Penuel, and slew the men of the city." SumBible reports the historical-political-action register per §0 Prime Directive #5; the canonical narrative records the actions without theological-commentary, and the cycle's deteriorative-trajectory frame (apostasy onset in the next-generation; the ephod-compromise immediately following) operates as the broader interpretive context.

**The kings' execution** (8:18-21). Zebah and Zalmunna identify the men they had slain at Tabor as resembling "the children of a king" — Gideon's brothers (8:18-19). Gideon's command to his firstborn Jether at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={8} verse={20} /> ("Up, and slay them") goes unfulfilled because "the youth drew not his sword: for he feared, because he was yet a youth." The kings themselves request Gideon do it at 8:21 ("Rise thou, and fall upon us: for as the man is, so is his strength"); Gideon executes them and takes "the ornaments that were on their camels' necks."

**The anti-monarchy declaration** (8:22-23). Israel's offer at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={8} verse={22} /> registers the canonical narrative's first request for hereditary-monarchy: "Rule thou over us, both thou, and thy son, and thy son's son also: for thou hast delivered us from the hand of Midian." Gideon's refusal at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={8} verse={23} /> is the chapter's structurally-decisive theological statement: "I will not rule over you, neither shall my son rule over you: the LORD shall rule over you." The declaration's significance is examined in the LangNote below.

**The ephod compromise** (8:24-27). Immediately after the anti-monarchy declaration, Gideon makes a request that becomes the chapter's theological-counterpoint at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={8} verse={24} />: "I would desire a request of you, that ye would give me every man the earrings of his prey." The Israelites comply willingly; the total comes to 1,700 shekels of gold (8:26) plus ornaments, collars, purple raiment, and chains from the camels' necks. Gideon's making of an ephod from the spoil at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={8} verse={27} /> receives the chapter's clearest text-internal negative evaluation: "And Gideon made an ephod thereof, and put it in his city, even in Ophrah: and all Israel went thither a whoring after it: which thing became a snare unto Gideon, and to his house." The Heb. *moqesh* ("snare") is the same word used at Exod 23:33 (Session 23 Sub-Batch 5) and Judg 2:3 (Session 24 Sub-Batch 1) for the false-worship snares the LORD had warned against — now operating not from the remnant-nations' continued presence but from Israel's own deliverer's compromise.

**Rest + Gideon's death** (8:28-32). The cycle closes formally at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={8} verse={28} />: "Thus was Midian subdued before the children of Israel, so that they lifted up their heads no more. And the country was in quietness forty years in the days of Gideon." The Gideon-life summary at 8:30-31 notes 70 sons "of his body begotten: for he had many wives" + Abimelech via "his concubine that was in Shechem" — preparing the Abimelech narrative at Judges 9. Gideon dies "in a good old age" (8:32).

**The cyclic-apostasy-onset** (8:33-35). The cycle's deteriorative-trajectory completes immediately at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={8} verse="33-35" />: "And it came to pass, as soon as Gideon was dead, that the children of Israel turned again, and went a whoring after Baalim, and made Baalberith their god, And the children of Israel remembered not the LORD their God, who had delivered them out of the hands of all their enemies on every side: Neither shewed they kindness to the house of Jerubbaal, namely, Gideon, according to all the goodness which he had shewed unto Israel." The Baalberith ("Lord of the covenant") cult-installation at Shechem prepares the Judges 9 Abimelech narrative; the no-kindness-to-the-house-of-Jerubbaal notation prepares Abimelech's subsequent slaughter of his 70 brothers (Judg 9:5). The Judg 2:19 framework-statement ("when the judge was dead, that they returned, and corrupted themselves more than their fathers" — drafted Session 24 SB1) operates here at the cycle's specific apostasy-onset register.

**Heb 11:32 + 11:34 faith-paradigm reading vs the Judg 8 ephod-compromise reality — the productive theological tension reported without arbitration.** Gideon is the first of the four Judges-figures named in the Hebrews 11:32 faith-paradigm cloud-of-witnesses: "And what shall I more say? for the time would fail me to tell of Gedeon, and of Barak, and of Samson, and of Jephthae" (KJV, fetch-verified Session 24 SB3). The Heb 11:34 general description — "out of weakness were made strong, waxed valiant in fight, turned to flight the armies of the aliens" (fetch-verified Session 24 SB3) — fits the Gideon cycle precisely at multiple points: "out of weakness were made strong" at Judg 6:15's self-description ("my family is poor in Manasseh, and I am the least in my father's house") and the 22,000 → 300 reduction at Judg 7:1-8; "waxed valiant in fight" at Judg 7:19-22's trumpets-and-torches assault; "turned to flight the armies of the aliens" at Judg 7:21-22's Midianite-host flight and the Judg 8 pursuit of Zebah and Zalmunna.

The faith-paradigm reading sits in productive theological tension with the Judg 8 ephod-compromise reality. Three structural elements of the tension:

(1) **The ephod-compromise's text-internal negative evaluation.** The Judg 8:27 evaluation ("all Israel went thither a whoring after it: which thing became a snare unto Gideon, and to his house") is the chapter's clearest negative-judgment. The *moqesh* (snare) terminology operates at the same register as Exod 23:33 (the Pentateuchal warning that the remnant-nations' gods would be a snare) and Judg 2:3 (the angel of the LORD's warning at Bochim — drafted Session 24 SB1): the snare-framework has now turned from external-cause (the remnant-nations) to internal-cause (Israel's deliverer's compromise). The compromise is theologically substantive within the chapter's own framing.

(2) **The cycle's deteriorative-trajectory closure at 8:33-35.** Israel returns to Baalim immediately after Gideon's death; the "made Baalberith their god" notation registers a worse apostasy than the Baal-altar Gideon's call narrative had begun by destroying (Judg 6:25-32). The Judg 2:19 framework-statement ("more than their fathers" — drafted Session 24 SB1) is operating: this cycle's apostasy-onset is worse than the prior cycle's. Gideon's leadership has not prevented the cycle's deterioration; the ephod-compromise has contributed to it.

(3) **The Heb 11:32 register-distinction handling.** The Hebrews-author's selection of Gideon for the faith-paradigm cluster operates at the faith-register — what Gideon trusted in (the LORD's promise of deliverance via 300 against 135,000) and how he acted on that trust (the trumpets-and-torches assault). The moral-evaluation register that the Judg 8 chapter's ephod-compromise opens is not the Hebrews-author's selection-frame. The two registers are not contradictory; they apply to the same person at different evaluation-axes. The faith-paradigm cluster reads what was trusted; the moral-evaluation register reads what was done with the prosperity that followed. SumBible reports the register-distinction per §0 Prime Directive #5 — both registers operate simultaneously and the canonical narrative carries both as legitimate without resolution.

The same register-distinction operates on Barak (faith-register at Heb 11:32 vs the moral-evaluation register some commentary positions read at Judg 4:8 — drafted Session 24 SB3), and will operate on Samson and Jephthah (Heb 11:32 cluster's third and fourth members; future-session chapters where the faith-register vs moral-evaluation register tension will be most-pronounced). The cluster's structural significance: the Hebrews-author selects four figures whose Judges-narrative carries substantial moral-complexity, and reads them at the faith-register the canonical narrative itself does not always foreground. The reading is theological-typological at the NT-canonical-redaction level, not exegetical-arbitration of the OT-narrative's evaluation.

**The Judg 8:22-23 anti-monarchy declaration + the cross-corpus LORD-shall-rule-over-you framework.** The anti-monarchy declaration is the canonical narrative's clearest pre-Samuel articulation of the LORD-shall-rule-over-you framework. Three structural elements compose its significance:

(1) **Israel's first canonical request for hereditary-monarchy.** The offer at 8:22 ("Rule thou over us, both thou, and thy son, and thy son's son also") is the canonical-OT's first request for a dynastic-rule arrangement — predating the 1 Samuel 8 request to Samuel by approximately one generation. The request's specifically-dynastic register (the named three-generation succession) registers as the substance of what Gideon refuses; he refuses not only personal-rule but the establishment of a Gideon-dynasty.

(2) **Gideon's refusal as preservation of the Exod 19:5-6 + Deut 17:14-20 frameworks.** The Exod 19:5-6 framework ("Ye shall be unto me a kingdom of priests, and an holy nation") had installed Israel's identity as the LORD's-own-people directly-ruled by the LORD. The Deut 17:14-20 framework had permitted the eventual installation of a human king but only under conditions that subordinated the king to the LORD's-direct-rule (the king reading the law-of-Moses daily; not multiplying horses, wives, or gold). Gideon's refusal at 8:23 preserves the Exod 19 register at its strongest reading — the LORD shall rule, not the Gideon-dynasty.

(3) **The immediate-tension test-case at Judges 9 — Abimelech's inversion.** Gideon refuses dynastic-rule; his son by the Shechem concubine seeks it in Shechem. The Gideon-Abimelech father-son pair functions as the canonical narrative's first deliberate test-case for the LORD-shall-rule-over-you framework: the father refuses, the son inverts. The 1 Samuel 8 request to Samuel — Israel asking for a king "like all the nations" — operates as the framework's second-canonical-iteration; Samuel's resistance, the LORD's allowance, and Saul's installation together form the framework's eventual-resolution within the Deuteronomistic History.

The chapter's juxtaposition is the most-discussed single structural-irony in the Gideon cycle: in the same chapter Gideon refuses kingship in the LORD's name and then makes an ephod that becomes a *moqesh* (snare). The two acts together register the cycle's deteriorative complexity — Gideon's theological-clarity on the monarchy-question coexisting with his cultic-compromise on the ephod-question. The Judges-narrative does not resolve the tension; the canonical narrative carries it forward to the Abimelech narrative at Judges 9, where the chapter's tensions land as immediate-historical-political-consequence.
