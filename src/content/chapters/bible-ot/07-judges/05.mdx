Judges 5 is the Song of Deborah — the poetic-register witness to the same Kishon deliverance the prior chapter's prose-narrative recorded. The chapter is one of the oldest extended Hebrew poetic compositions in the canon, commonly dated by scholarly consensus on linguistic grounds to the twelfth century BCE (see LangNote below). Six major movements compose the song.

**Preamble + Sinai-theophany imagery** (5:1-5). The chapter opens at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={5} verse={1} />: "Then sang Deborah and Barak the son of Abinoam on that day, saying," — the rare dual-attribution of a song to two named figures, with the Song's poetic content following.

<ScriptureBlock book="judges" chapter={5} verses="2-5">

Praise ye the LORD for the avenging of Israel, when the people willingly offered themselves.

Hear, O ye kings; give ear, O ye princes; I, even I, will sing unto the LORD; I will sing praise to the LORD God of Israel.

LORD, when thou wentest out of Seir, when thou marchedst out of the field of Edom, the earth trembled, and the heavens dropped, the clouds also dropped water.

The mountains melted from before the LORD, even that Sinai from before the LORD God of Israel.

</ScriptureBlock>

The opening summons (5:3 — "Hear, O ye kings; give ear, O ye princes") addresses kings and princes broadly, framing the song as a public-witness to a divine-act with cosmic significance. The theophany-imagery at 5:4-5 invokes the Sinai-tradition language — the LORD coming from Seir / Edom, the earth tremor, the mountains melting — that recurs across the OT's theophany-poetry corpus (cf. Exod 15:5-8 + Hab 3:3-6 + Ps 68:7-8). The chapter's theophany positions the Kishon deliverance within the broader pattern of the LORD's covenantal-acts since Sinai.

**Pre-Deborah desolation + Deborah's rise** (5:6-8). The Song then narrates the pre-deliverance condition at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={5} verse="6-7" />: "In the days of Shamgar the son of Anath, in the days of Jael, the highways were unoccupied, and the travellers walked through byways. The inhabitants of the villages ceased, they ceased in Israel, until that I Deborah arose, that I arose a mother in Israel." The Heb. *em b'yisrael* ("mother in Israel") is a distinctive title applied to Deborah here for the first time in canonical narrative — the title recurs as a generalized honorific term at 2 Sam 20:19 (the wise woman of Abel of Beth-maachah), but Deborah's instance at 5:7 is the title's first attested use as a personal-political-prophetic designation. The diagnosis at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={5} verse={8} /> ("They chose new gods; then was war in the gates: was there a shield or spear seen among forty thousand in Israel?") attributes the desolation to apostasy (the cyclic-pattern's "did evil" stage) and registers the military-incapacity that followed.

**Tribal participation roll-call** (5:9-18). The Song's longest movement is the tribal-participation evaluation. Deborah's heart-attitude at 5:9 ("My heart is toward the governors of Israel, that offered themselves willingly among the people") sets the celebratory frame for the commended tribes; the "Awake, awake" address at 5:12 ("Awake, awake, Deborah: awake, awake, utter a song: arise, Barak, and lead thy captivity captive, thou son of Abinoam") opens the roll-call proper.

Six tribes are commended for participation: Ephraim (5:14, "Out of Ephraim was there a root of them against Amalek"); Benjamin (5:14, "after thee, Benjamin, among thy people"); Machir = Manasseh (5:14, "out of Machir came down governors"); Zebulun (5:14, "out of Zebulun they that handle the pen of the writer"); Issachar (5:15, "the princes of Issachar were with Deborah; even Issachar, and also Barak"); Naphtali (5:18, "Zebulun and Naphtali were a people that jeoparded their lives unto the death in the high places of the field"). Judg 4:6 (prior chapter, SB3 block) had specified only Naphtali and Zebulun as Barak's directly-mustered ten thousand; the Song's expanded roll-call honors the additional participating tribes.

Four tribes are rebuked for absence, each with a brief explanation that registers as gentle-mockery: Reuben at 5:15-16 ("Why abodest thou among the sheepfolds, to hear the bleatings of the flocks? For the divisions of Reuben there were great searchings of heart"); Gilead at 5:17 ("Gilead abode beyond Jordan" — the trans-Jordan Gad-and-half-Manasseh territory); Dan at 5:17 ("and why did Dan remain in ships?" — Dan's coastal territory, anticipating the Dan-relocation narrative at Judg 18); Asher at 5:17 ("Asher continued on the sea shore, and abode in his breaches"). The roll-call's structural-form is one of the OT's earliest enumerative-poetic forms (see LangNote 3 below).

**The cosmic-battle at Megiddo + Kishon + horse-hooves** (5:19-22). The battle-poetry intensifies the prose-narrative's account at Judg 4:14-16.

<ScriptureBlock book="judges" chapter={5} verses="19-22">

The kings came and fought, then fought the kings of Canaan in Taanach by the waters of Megiddo; they took no gain of money.

They fought from heaven; the stars in their courses fought against Sisera.

The river of Kishon swept them away, that ancient river, the river Kishon. O my soul, thou hast trodden down strength.

Then were the horsehoofs broken by the means of the pransings, the pransings of their mighty ones.

</ScriptureBlock>

Three poetic-imagery moves distinguish this movement. The "stars in their courses" at 5:20 registers the deliverance at the cosmic-warfare register — the LORD's hosts include the heavenly bodies fighting on Israel's behalf. The "river of Kishon swept them away" at 5:21 is the iron-chariots framework's poetic-resolution image — the river's flooding rendered the chariot-advantage useless, exactly as the iron-chariots-framework had been narratively-resolved at Judg 4:15 (drafted SB3 prior block; see Judg 4 vLog #4 on the cross-session resolution). The "horsehoofs broken" at 5:22 closes the battle-poetry with the chariot-horses' own self-destruction.

**Curse of Meroz + Jael celebration** (5:23-27). The song's centerpiece begins with the curse of Meroz at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={5} verse={23} />: "Curse ye Meroz, said the angel of the LORD, curse ye bitterly the inhabitants thereof; because they came not to the help of the LORD, to the help of the LORD against the mighty." Meroz is an otherwise-unknown location; the curse's structural-significance is its parallelism with the Jael blessing that follows immediately — non-participation cursed; participation blessed-above-women.

<ScriptureBlock book="judges" chapter={5} verses="24-27">

Blessed above women shall Jael the wife of Heber the Kenite be, blessed shall she be above women in the tent.

He asked water, and she gave him milk; she brought forth butter in a lordly dish.

She put her hand to the nail, and her right hand to the workmen's hammer; and with the hammer she smote Sisera, she smote off his head, when she had pierced and stricken through his temples.

At her feet he bowed, he fell, he lay down: at her feet he bowed, he fell: where he bowed, there he fell down dead.

</ScriptureBlock>

The poetic-celebratory register of 5:24-27 intensifies the prose-narrative's Judg 4:21 account through three features (see LangNote 2 below): the blessing-formula at 5:24 (the strongest female-blessing in the canonical OT); the hospitality-detail intensification at 5:25 ("butter in a lordly dish" — the lavish hospitality-response heightens the hospitality-framework-tension); the chiastic killing-narrative at 5:27 ("at her feet he bowed, he fell, he lay down: at her feet he bowed, he fell: where he bowed, there he fell down dead"), employing intensive-poetic parallelism that registers the deed at the song's celebratory pole.

**Mother of Sisera + closing benediction** (5:28-31). The song closes with a dramatic-irony scene focused on Sisera's mother at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={5} verse={28} />: "The mother of Sisera looked out at a window, and cried through the lattice, Why is his chariot so long in coming? why tarry the wheels of his chariots?" The wise ladies' answer at 5:29-30 — that Sisera must be dividing the spoil among his warriors ("to every man a damsel or two; to Sisera a prey of divers colours") — registers as tragic-irony: the mother and her ladies imagine the deliverance Sisera himself was certain of, while the audience knows Sisera lies dead in Jael's tent. The closing benediction at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={5} verse={31} /> closes the song formally: "So let all thine enemies perish, O LORD: but let them that love him be as the sun when he goeth forth in his might. And the land had rest forty years." The "land had rest forty years" formula concludes the Deborah-Barak cycle's cyclic-pattern execution.

**The Song of Deborah as one of the oldest extended Hebrew poetic compositions — archaic-Hebrew features + the OT theophany-poetry trajectory.** The Song's dating is one of biblical scholarship's stronger consensus positions: most major commentaries (Boling, Webb, Soggin, Alter) date the Song's composition to roughly the twelfth century BCE on linguistic grounds — corresponding closely to the historical period the narrative depicts. The dating-argument is based on identifiable archaic-Hebrew grammatical features that distinguish the Song from later Hebrew prose-style: distinctive verbal forms, archaic suffix usage, vocabulary preserved in poetic-formulaic contexts but absent from later Hebrew, and parallelism patterns that match other proposed early-monarchic-and-pre-monarchic Hebrew poetic compositions (Exod 15 Song of the Sea; Gen 49 Jacob's tribal blessings; possibly Deut 33 Moses's tribal blessings and Ps 68). SumBible reports the consensus per §0 Prime Directive #5 without arbitrating among the specific linguistic-dating positions.

The Song's theophany-imagery at 5:4-5 (the LORD coming from Seir and Edom; earth tremor; mountains melting; Sinai-tradition language) positions the Kishon deliverance within an OT theophany-poetry trajectory that includes Exod 15:5-8 (the Song of the Sea's poetic-recounting of the Red Sea crossing); Hab 3:3-6 (the LORD coming "from Teman, and the Holy One from mount Paran" with the same earth-tremor and mountain-melting imagery); Ps 68:7-8 (the LORD going "before thy people... the earth shook, the heavens also dropped at the presence of God: even Sinai itself was moved at the presence of God"). The shared imagery across these passages installs a recurring OT-poetic motif: the LORD's covenantal-acts are framed at the cosmic register, with creation itself participating in the divine-deliverance. The Judges 5:4-5 instance is one of the earliest of these four; the dating-consensus places it earlier than the Hab 3 and Ps 68 instances and roughly contemporary with the Exod 15 instance.

**The Jael celebration's dual-register handling — poetic-celebratory pole vs prose-narrative-recording pole.** The Song's 5:24-27 Jael-celebration and the Judg 4:17-22 prose Jael-narrative together form one of the canonical narrative's most distinctive single-event dual-register witnesses. The interpretive spectrum reported at the Judg 4 LangNote (SB3 prior block) — heroic-deliverance / morally-problematic-deception / ANE-hospitality-violation-as-narrative-significance / sacred-narrative-recording-without-endorsing — operates across both registers, with each register positioning differently within the spectrum.

The Judg 4 prose register operates at the narrative-recording pole: the event is reported with prophecy-fulfillment framing (Judg 4:9's "the LORD shall sell Sisera into the hand of a woman") and theological-attribution (Judg 4:23's "So God subdued on that day Jabin"), but the methodology-details are described in matter-of-fact narrative terms. The Judg 5 poetic register operates at the celebratory pole: the blessing-formula at 5:24 ("Blessed above women shall Jael the wife of Heber the Kenite be") is the strongest female-blessing in the canonical OT; the hospitality-detail intensification at 5:25 foregrounds the hospitality-framework register as deliberate-and-extensive; the chiastic killing-narrative at 5:27 employs intensive-poetic parallelism that the reader's-ear registers as celebratory-emphasis. The dual-register witness preserves the interpretive spectrum without resolving it — different registers position the act differently, and the canonical narrative carries both registers as legitimate witness.

The Lucan Magnificat at Luke 1:42's Elizabeth-blessing of Mary — "Blessed art thou among women" — shares the formal female-blessing structure though through the LXX Greek's *eulogemene en gynaixin* rather than direct OT citation. The canonical-corpus female-blessing-formula register extends from Judg 5:24 (Jael) through later instances and finds its NT-reception in the Mary-blessing — the trajectory is form-shared rather than text-citation-shared. The Hannah's-prayer at 1 Sam 2:1-10 sits between these as another major female-prophetic-celebratory composition; the Hannah → Magnificat trajectory is the most-discussed OT-NT female-prophetic-prayer parallel in NT scholarship.

**The tribal-participation roll-call at 5:13-18 — one of the OT's earliest enumerative-poetic forms + the canonical narrative's tribal-evaluation tradition.** The Song's tribal-roll-call form sits within an OT enumerative-poetic tradition that includes Gen 49 (Jacob's tribal blessings at the patriarchal-prophecy register), Deut 33 (Moses's tribal blessings at the covenantal-farewell register), and the later Ps 68:27 brief tribal-procession reference. The Judges 5 instance is distinctive in its evaluation register — the roll-call evaluates tribal participation in a specific deliverance-event rather than characterizing tribal-identity per se. The structural-form's significance: the Song treats tribal-participation as the LORD's covenantal-test that operates at the tribal-collective level, not only at the individual level. The four absent tribes (Reuben, Gilead/Gad, Dan, Asher) are each given a brief explanation that the form registers as gentle-mockery; the six present tribes are honored, with Zebulun and Naphtali receiving the strongest commendation at 5:18 ("Zebulun and Naphtali were a people that jeoparded their lives unto the death in the high places of the field") since they were Barak's directly-mustered ten thousand at Judg 4:6.

The Dan-in-ships notation at 5:17 — "why did Dan remain in ships?" — is geographically-significant: it locates Dan at the chapter's compositional moment in coastal-shipping territory, anticipating the Dan-relocation narrative at Judg 18 where Dan moves inland to take Laish/Leshem (renamed Dan; the relocation drafted at Josh 19:47 Session 23 Sub-Batch 3 with the "from Dan to Beersheba" formula context). The Song's tribal-roll-call thereby preserves an early-Israelite geographic snapshot before Dan's later inland-relocation; this is one of the dating-consensus's strongest internal-evidence anchors for the Song's archaic composition.
