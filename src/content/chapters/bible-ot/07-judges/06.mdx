Judges 6 opens the Gideon cycle — the fourth major-judge cycle (after Othniel, Ehud, Deborah-Barak) and the longest single judge-narrative since Joshua. Six movements structure the chapter: the Midianite oppression (6:1-6); the unnamed-prophet's covenantal-lawsuit (6:7-10); the angel of the LORD theophany at Ophrah (6:11-24); the Baal-altar destruction and Jerubbaal-naming (6:25-32); the Spirit-empowerment and trumpet rallying (6:33-35); the two-stage fleece sign sequence (6:36-40).

**The Midianite oppression** (6:1-6). The cyclic-pattern's apostasy-stage opens at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={6} verse={1} />: "And the children of Israel did evil in the sight of the LORD: and the LORD delivered them into the hand of Midian seven years." The oppression's distinctive feature is its agricultural-destruction character — the Midianites and Amalekites and "children of the east" came up at harvest time, destroyed the increase of the earth "till thou come unto Gaza," and "left no sustenance for Israel, neither sheep, nor ox, nor ass" (6:4). The Israelites' response at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={6} verse={2} /> was to "make them the dens which are in the mountains, and caves, and strong holds" — hiding rather than confronting. The cry unto the LORD comes at 6:6.

**The unnamed-prophet's covenantal-lawsuit** (6:7-10). The cycle's distinctive interpretive element is a prophet sent before the deliverer — a covenantal-lawsuit reframing the oppression at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={6} verse="8-10" />: "I brought you up from Egypt, and brought you forth out of the house of bondage; And I delivered you out of the hand of the Egyptians, and out of the hand of all that oppressed you, and drave them out from before you, and gave you their land; And I said unto you, I am the LORD your God; fear not the gods of the Amorites, in whose land ye dwell: but ye have not obeyed my voice." The lawsuit's three-element structure — recap of the LORD's redemptive-acts; recap of the LORD's covenantal-stipulation; statement of Israel's failure — recurs Joshua 24:2-13 (Session 23) and Judges 2:1-3 (Bochim, Session 24) and operates as one of the OT's recurring covenantal-judgment forms.

**The angel of the LORD theophany at Ophrah** (6:11-24). The scene opens at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={6} verse={11} />: "And there came an angel of the LORD, and sat under an oak which was in Ophrah, that pertained unto Joash the Abiezrite: and his son Gideon threshed wheat by the winepress, to hide it from the Midianites." Two narrative details prepare the scene's reading: the angel's seated position under the oak (a posture of waiting rather than appearing-and-departing); and Gideon's hiding-while-threshing posture (the routine activity of harvest reduced to covert-operation by the oppression). The greeting at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={6} verse={12} /> — "The LORD is with thee, thou mighty man of valour" — is ironic in two registers: Gideon's hidden-threshing has no obvious valor; the LORD's withness is the very thing Gideon's protest will question.

The dialogue follows the OT theophany-commissioning form (see LangNote below). Gideon's protest at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={6} verse={13} /> raises the cycle's interpretive question: "if the LORD be with us, why then is all this befallen us? and where be all his miracles which our fathers told us of, saying, Did not the LORD bring us up from Egypt? but now the LORD hath forsaken us." The commissioning at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={6} verse={14} /> shifts speaker-designation from "angel of the LORD" to "the LORD" without explanation: "And the LORD looked upon him, and said, Go in this thy might, and thou shalt save Israel from the hand of the Midianites: have not I sent thee?" Gideon's second protest at 6:15 ("my family is poor in Manasseh, and I am the least in my father's house") mirrors the Moses-protest at Exod 3:11 (see LangNote below); the LORD's response at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={6} verse={16} /> ("Surely I will be with thee, and thou shalt smite the Midianites as one man") mirrors the Exod 3:12 "Certainly I will be with thee" response.

The sign-request and sign-giving at 6:17-21 close the commissioning. Gideon prepares a meal (a kid, unleavened cakes from an ephah of flour, broth); the angel directs him to place the flesh and cakes on the rock and pour out the broth; the angel touches them with the staff in his hand; "there rose up fire out of the rock, and consumed the flesh and the unleavened cakes. Then the angel of the LORD departed out of his sight" (6:21). Gideon's response at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={6} verse={22} /> registers the holy-fear: "Alas, O Lord GOD! for because I have seen an angel of the LORD face to face." The LORD's reassurance at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={6} verse={23} /> closes the theophany: "Peace be unto thee; fear not: thou shalt not die." Gideon then builds an altar at the site and names it <LangNote term="Jehovah-shalom" script="יְהוָה שָׁלוֹם" language="Hebrew" gloss="‘The LORD is peace.’ Gideon's altar-naming at Judg 6:24 follows the OT pattern of altar-and-place naming that records a divine encounter or revelation — paralleling Jacob's Bethel ('house of God') at Gen 28:19, Moses's Jehovah-nissi ('the LORD is my banner') at Exod 17:15, and the chapter at hand's Jerubbaal naming later in the chapter. The peace-naming here registers the LORD's reassurance at 6:23 ('Peace be unto thee; fear not: thou shalt not die') as the encounter's structural-resolution.">Jehovah-shalom</LangNote> ("the LORD is peace").

**The Baal-altar destruction + Jerubbaal-naming** (6:25-32). That same night the LORD commands Gideon to throw down his father's altar of Baal and the Asherah-grove beside it, and to build an altar to the LORD in their place — sacrificing his father's seven-year-old bullock as the burnt offering with the grove's wood (6:25-26). Gideon does it at night with ten servants "because he feared his father's household, and the men of the city" (6:27). The morning discovery rouses the city against him; his father Joash defends him at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={6} verse={31} /> with a theological argument: "Will ye plead for Baal? will ye save him? he that will plead for him, let him be put to death whilst it is yet morning: if he be a god, let him plead for himself, because one hath cast down his altar." The Jerubbaal-naming at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={6} verse={32} /> ("Let Baal plead against him") becomes Gideon's alternative name across the rest of the cycle and into Judges 9 (Abimelech is "the son of Jerubbaal").

**Spirit-empowerment + trumpet rallying** (6:33-35). The Midianite-Amalekite-Easterner coalition gathers in the valley of Jezreel; the response at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={6} verse={34} /> is: "But the Spirit of the LORD came upon Gideon, and he blew a trumpet; and Abiezer was gathered after him." The Spirit-empowerment formula recurs from the Othniel cycle (Judg 3:10, drafted Session 24 SB2) and will recur at Jephthah (Judg 11:29) and Samson (Judg 13:25, 14:6, 14:19, 15:14). Gideon's messengers gather Manasseh, Asher, Zebulun, and Naphtali — four of the same tribes the Song of Deborah commended (Judg 5:14-18, Session 24 SB4).

**The fleece sign sequence** (6:36-40). The cycle closes with Gideon's two-stage sign request at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={6} verse="36-40" />. The first sign (wet fleece, dry ground) is asked at 6:37 and granted at 6:38. The second sign (dry fleece, dewy ground) is asked at 6:39 with explicit acknowledgment of the request's audacity ("Let not thine anger be hot against me, and I will speak but this once: let me prove, I pray thee, but this once with the fleece") and granted at 6:40. The double-fleece sequence is one of the OT's most-discussed sign-and-confirmation episodes; commentary positions on Gideon's repeated sign-asking run across the interpretive spectrum from faith-seeking-confirmation (the LORD condescends to Gideon's weakness as pastoral pedagogy) to weakness-of-faith-bordering-on-testing-God (the request after the angel-theophany and the Spirit-coming is read as inadequately-trusting). The canonical narrative records both signs as granted without arbitrating among the interpretive positions; SumBible reports the spectrum per §0 Prime Directive #5.

**The angel of the LORD (mal'akh YHWH) theophany framework — multi-position commentary spectrum reported without arbitration.** The Judg 6:11-24 theophany sits at the heart of one of the OT-narrative's most-discussed Christian-and-Jewish interpretive frameworks. Four position-types operate across the reception-history, consistent with the spectrum reported at Joshua 5:13-15 (drafted Session 21):

(1) **Christophany / pre-incarnate Christ reading.** The figure receives Gideon's offering at 6:18-21 (touched by the staff, the fire rising and consuming) — an action paralleling later sacrificial-reception by the LORD; Gideon addresses him as *adonai YHWH* ("Lord GOD") at 6:22 in the same form Abraham addresses the LORD at Gen 15:2, 8; the speaker-designation shifts from "angel of the LORD" (6:11, 12) to "the LORD" (6:14, 16) without narrative-explanation, and the first-person LORD-speech ("Surely I will be with thee, and thou shalt smite the Midianites as one man" — 6:16; "Peace be unto thee; fear not: thou shalt not die" — 6:23) operates in the LORD's own voice. Substantial Christian-commentary reception (patristic, medieval, Reformation, and post-Reformation evangelical) reads mal'akh-YHWH instances as pre-incarnate Christophany on the ground that the figure speaks-and-acts in the LORD's own register while being distinguishable from the LORD-as-Father; the framework parallels the wisdom-Christology and the Logos-Christology developed in later Christian theology.

(2) **Angelophany / divine-messenger reading.** The figure is explicitly designated "an angel of the LORD" at 6:11 and 6:21; the OT mal'akh-YHWH framework (Gen 16:7-13 Hagar at the well; Gen 22:11-18 Abraham at Moriah; Exod 3:2-6 the burning bush; Num 22:22-35 Balaam's donkey; Zechariah 1-6 the night visions) registers the angel of the LORD as the LORD's appointed-and-empowered messenger speaking on the LORD's behalf with the LORD's full authority. The distinct-but-closely-identified relationship (the messenger speaks the sender's words in the sender's voice; the boundary between messenger and sender is functionally minimal in the chapter at hand) is the framework's structural feature. On this reading, the mal'akh-YHWH is created and angelic, but the messenger's word IS the LORD's word in the standard ANE messenger-paradigm.

(3) **Commander-of-heavenly-host reading.** The Joshua 5:13-15 sar-tseva-YHWH framework (the captain of the host of the LORD, drafted Session 21) operates as the closest in-canon parallel; the Judg 6 angel of the LORD here is read as a member or commander of the heavenly host functionally equivalent to the captain who appeared to Joshua. This reading grounds the angelophany in the broader OT cosmic-military framework (cf. 1 Kings 22:19-23 the heavenly host before the LORD's throne; Psalm 103:21 "Bless ye the LORD, all ye his hosts; ye ministers of his, that do his pleasure").

(4) **Jewish-tradition readings of mal'akh-YHWH as the LORD's appointed angelic messenger** operating within angelological tradition without Christological reading. The position holds the mal'akh-YHWH framework's distinct-from-the-LORD register and reads the first-person LORD-speech in the messenger's mouth as a feature of the ANE messenger-paradigm rather than as identity with the LORD.

The chapter at hand's distinctive text-internal data contributes to the spectrum: the speaker-designation shifts twice (6:11-13 "angel of the LORD"; 6:14-18 "the LORD"; 6:20 "the angel of God"; 6:21-22 "the angel of the LORD"; 6:23 "the LORD" again) — and the canonical narrative offers no explanation of the shifting designations. The chapter preserves both registers — the angel-distinct-from-the-LORD register and the LORD-himself register — simultaneously. SumBible reports the spectrum per §0 Prime Directive #5 (when uncertain, omit arbitration); the chapter's distinctive contribution is not a resolution of the spectrum but the preservation of both registers as the canonical narrative carries them.

The cross-corpus theophany-commissioning trajectory in which this scene participates has four major OT-narrative instances: Exod 3:1-15 (Moses at the burning bush at Horeb); Joshua 5:13-15 (Joshua at Jericho with the captain of the LORD's host); the chapter at hand (Gideon at Ophrah under the oak); Judges 13:2-23 (Manoah and his wife at the announcement of Samson's birth — future-session reference). The four scenes share recurring structural features that the LangNote vLog #3 enumerates: appearance to a reluctant figure at ordinary occupation; commissioning with protest of unworthiness; promise of divine-presence as sufficient warrant; sign requested and granted; holy-fear at perceiving the LORD's presence. The recurring pattern operates as the OT-narrative's standard theophany-commissioning form, with each instance contributing distinctive elements: Moses' staff-and-leprous-hand signs; Joshua's holy-ground command paralleling Exod 3:5; Gideon's fire-from-the-rock sign + Jehovah-shalom altar-naming; Manoah's flame-ascending sign + the angel's wonderful-name reply.

The substantial Christian-commentary reception of the mal'akh-YHWH framework as Christophany-anchor in OT-NT typology operates as one of the historic Christian theological-trajectories the reception-history records; SumBible reports the position alongside the angelophany / divine-messenger / Jewish-tradition positions without arbitration. The canonical narrative's own framing preserves the distinct-but-closely-identified relationship without resolution; the historic interpretive positions extend that text-internal preservation into systematic-theological territory in directions the text itself does not specify.
