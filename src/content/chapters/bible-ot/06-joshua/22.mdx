Joshua 22 narrates a crisis resolved through dialogue rather than war. Four movements: Joshua dismisses the two-and-a-half eastern tribes home (22:1-9); they build a great altar at the Jordan crossing and the western tribes mobilize against them (22:10-12); a priest-led delegation investigates (22:13-20); the eastern tribes explain the altar's true purpose and the crisis resolves (22:21-34).

**Joshua dismisses the eastern tribes** (22:1-9). The chapter opens at <VerseRef book="joshua" chapter={22} verse="1-2" />: "Then Joshua called the Reubenites, and the Gadites, and the half tribe of Manasseh, And said unto them, Ye have kept all that Moses the servant of the LORD commanded you, and have obeyed my voice in all that I commanded you." The dismissal carries forward the obligation framework set at Num 32 (the eastern tribes' deal with Moses: receive trans-Jordan inheritance now, but go armed across with the western tribes until the cis-Jordan conquest is complete). Joshua certifies the deal kept. He then issues a covenantal-charge at <VerseRef book="joshua" chapter={22} verse={5} />: "But take diligent heed to do the commandment and the law, which Moses the servant of the LORD charged you, to love the LORD your God, and to walk in all his ways, and to keep his commandments, and to cleave unto him, and to serve him with all your heart and with all your soul." The five-verb charge — love, walk, keep, cleave, serve — recapitulates the Deuteronomic loyalty-vocabulary in compressed form.

**The altar at the Jordan + the war-mobilization** (22:10-12). The eastern tribes depart, but at <VerseRef book="joshua" chapter={22} verse={10} /> they "built there an altar by Jordan, a great altar to see to" — a deliberately imposing structure. The western tribes interpret this geographically: they are still inside the cis-Jordan territory ("over against the land of Canaan, in the borders of Jordan, at the passage of the children of Israel"), which to the western tribes can only mean a rival sacrificial-altar set up to compete with the legitimate Shiloh tabernacle. The whole congregation at <VerseRef book="joshua" chapter={22} verse={12} /> "gathered themselves together at Shiloh, to go up to war against them." The institutional discipline is to be noted: even the war-mobilization is staged at the cultic center, not improvised in the field.

**The priest-led investigation** (22:13-20). Rather than march immediately, the western tribes send a delegation: Phinehas son of Eleazar the priest, accompanied by ten princes (one for each cis-Jordan tribe — see <VerseRef book="joshua" chapter={22} verse={14} />). Phinehas's selection is significant beyond his priestly office: he was the same figure who at Num 25 halted the Peor plague through his zeal-for-the-LORD execution of Zimri and Cozbi (see LangNote below). The delegation's accusation at <VerseRef book="joshua" chapter={22} verse={16} /> is direct: "What trespass is this that ye have committed against the God of Israel, to turn away this day from following the LORD, in that ye have builded you an altar, that ye might rebel this day against the LORD?" The accusation invokes two prior collective-guilt precedents — the Peor episode at 22:17 and the Achan episode at <VerseRef book="joshua" chapter={22} verse={20} /> ("Did not Achan the son of Zerah commit a trespass in the accursed thing, and wrath fell on all the congregation of Israel? and that man perished not alone in his iniquity"). Notably the delegation also offers a constructive alternative at <VerseRef book="joshua" chapter={22} verse={19} />: if the eastern land is somehow unclean, the eastern tribes are invited to cross back and "take possession among us" rather than build a rival altar.

**The explanation and resolution** (22:21-34). The eastern tribes begin their response at <VerseRef book="joshua" chapter={22} verse={22} /> with a triple oath-formula: "The LORD God of gods, the LORD God of gods, he knoweth, and Israel he shall know; if it be in rebellion, or if in transgression against the LORD, (save us not this day,)." They submit to divine arbitration *before* explaining. The explanation follows at <VerseRef book="joshua" chapter={22} verse="24-28" />: they built the altar out of fear that, in generations to come, western descendants might say to eastern descendants, "What have ye to do with the LORD God of Israel? For the LORD hath made Jordan a border between us and you... ye have no part in the LORD." The altar is therefore a "witness" — a memorial-pattern of the legitimate altar at the tabernacle, deliberately not used for "burnt offerings, nor for sacrifices."

The resolution at <VerseRef book="joshua" chapter={22} verse="30-31" />: "And when Phinehas the priest, and the princes of the congregation and heads of the thousands of Israel which were with him, heard the words... it pleased them. And Phinehas the son of Eleazar the priest said... This day we perceive that the LORD is among us, because ye have not committed this trespass against the LORD: now ye have delivered the children of Israel out of the hand of the LORD." The chapter closes with the altar's naming at <VerseRef book="joshua" chapter={22} verse={34} />: "And the children of Reuben and the children of Gad called the altar Ed: for it shall be a witness between us that the LORD is God." The Hebrew *ed* means "witness."

<LangNotes>

**Conflict-resolution-through-dialogue: Phinehas's continuity from Num 25 to Josh 22.** The chapter's structural-significance lies in two overlapping frameworks — Phinehas's narrative-continuity, and the conflict-resolution-through-dialogue paradigm — both of which depend on the Num 25 (Session 15) precedent.

(1) **Phinehas's narrative-continuity.** Phinehas son of Eleazar is the priestly figure whose narrative-trajectory bridges the late Pentateuchal period and the late Joshua period. At Num 25 he executed Zimri and Cozbi in their tent — an act the LORD explicitly approved with the covenant-of-peace and an everlasting-priesthood promise (Num 25:11-13). At Josh 22 he is sent as the priestly-investigator of a potentially analogous covenantal-breach. The narrative-architecture's choice of Phinehas for this delegation is deliberate: he is the figure historically associated with zeal-for-the-LORD intervention against apostasy, and the chapter at 22:17 has him invoking his own prior episode ("Is the iniquity of Peor too little for us...") as the rhetorical precedent. The structural difference between Num 25 and Josh 22 is decisive: in Num 25 the apostasy was real and the response was immediate and lethal; in Josh 22 the apparent-apostasy is investigated, dialogue is offered, and the apparent-trespass turns out to be a misunderstanding. The two episodes together establish Phinehas as the priestly figure capable of *both* immediate zeal-judgment (when warranted) *and* careful investigation (when warranted) — the priestly discernment that distinguishes which response a given situation calls for.

(2) **The conflict-resolution-through-dialogue paradigm.** The chapter's structural pattern is one of the OT's most carefully-developed early-models for resolving inter-tribal religious conflict without civil war. Four institutional disciplines are visible:

(a) *Institutional channeling of alarm.* The western tribes do not improvise; they gather at the cultic center (Shiloh), which institutionalizes the response as a covenantal matter rather than a tribal-rivalry one.

(b) *Priest-led investigation as standard procedure.* The delegation is led by Phinehas, not by a military commander; the priestly office takes investigation-precedence over warfare-mobilization.

(c) *Accusation paired with constructive alternative.* The delegation at 22:19 offers the eastern tribes the option to cross back and "take possession among us" if their land is somehow unclean — a real alternative, not just an ultimatum. The constructive alternative leaves a graceful exit for the accused.

(d) *Triple oath-formula opening the defense.* The eastern tribes invoke divine arbitration ("the LORD God of gods, the LORD God of gods, he knoweth, and Israel he shall know") before explaining. The defense is theologically-framed, not tribally-defensive.

The single-altar discipline of Deut 12 is preserved throughout by both sides. The western tribes' alarm presumes Deut 12; the eastern tribes' fear (that they might be excluded from the legitimate altar) and their solution (a deliberately non-sacrificial witness-altar) both presume Deut 12. The chapter is a worked example of Deut 12 functioning as institutional bedrock — its centralization-of-cult principle is what makes the rival-altar accusation intelligible and the witness-altar resolution acceptable.

A final structural note: the chapter's Achan-reference at 22:20 invokes Josh 7's collective-guilt principle (drafted Session 21) as the within-Joshua precedent. The Peor-and-Achan double-precedent rhetoric installs the conceptual framework that one tribe's apostasy can incur wrath on the whole congregation — making the western tribes' alarm intelligible as covenantal-responsibility rather than just political-suspicion. The same collective-guilt principle, conditionally-applied with investigation and dialogue, becomes the conceptual framework for the *prevention* of civil war when the investigation reveals no actual apostasy. The Josh 22 narrative is therefore not the negation of the Peor + Achan collective-guilt principle but its mature, institutionally-channeled application.
