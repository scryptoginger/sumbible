The Book of Mormon is the keystone scripture of the Latter-day Saint tradition — a companion volume to the Bible rather than a replacement for it. Its 1982 subtitle, "Another Testament of Jesus Christ," states its canonical claim plainly: it offers a second witness, alongside the Bible, of the same Christ.

The book presents itself as the religious history of peoples living anciently in the Americas, written and kept across centuries by a succession of prophets. Most of its fifteen books were abridged from larger records by a prophet-historian named Mormon, for whom the volume is named; his son Moroni completed and concluded it. The internal narrative is peopled by several groups — chiefly the Nephites and Lamanites, descended from a family that left Jerusalem; the people of Zarahemla; and, in the embedded book of Ether, the much earlier Jaredites.

The story opens as the prophet Lehi, warned of Jerusalem's coming destruction, leads his family out of the city and eventually across the ocean to a new land (<VerseRef book="1-nephi" chapter={1} verse={1} />). What follows is a thousand-year cycle: a society prospers in righteousness, grows proud, fractures, repents, and prospers again. The narrative's center — the moment everything before anticipates and everything after remembers — is the descent of the resurrected Jesus Christ to a multitude gathered at the temple in the land Bountiful, where he introduces himself, teaches, and establishes his church (<VerseRef book="3-nephi" chapter={11} verse={10} />). After a brief golden age of unity, the peoples decline again, and the record closes with Moroni alone, burying the plates.

Its distinctive themes are continuous with the Bible's but pressed further: the Atonement of Christ reaching every people in every place; the covenant with the house of Israel understood as genuinely universal; the conviction that revelation did not cease with the biblical canon; and the gathering of Israel in the last days. The book ends by inviting each reader to test it directly, in prayer (<VerseRef book="moroni" chapter={10} verse="4-5" />).

How the Book of Mormon came to be is the question on which readers most divide. The Latter-day Saint tradition holds that Joseph Smith translated it from ancient golden plates "by the gift and power of God." The surrounding historical record includes its 1830 publication, Smith's accounts of receiving and translating the plates, and the published testimonies of the Three and Eight Witnesses who affirmed that they saw or handled them — alongside the fact that neither the plates nor any source-language manuscript is available for examination. Whether the book is an ancient record or a nineteenth-century one is a question the traditions answer very differently. SumBible reports the Latter-day Saint claim and the historical context side by side, and does not arbitrate between them.
