Four verses in <VerseRef book="genesis" chapter={5} verse="21-24" /> say that Enoch
walked with God and was not, for God took him. Almost everything else ever written
about Enoch grows out of that silence. By the last centuries before the common era
he had become the hero of an enormous literature — visions of heaven, the fall of
the Watchers, astronomical tables, a tour of the cosmos — that the earliest
Christians read as scripture and that was then almost completely destroyed.

In December 1830 Joseph Smith dictated what is now <VerseRef book="moses" chapter={6} /> and
<VerseRef book="moses" chapter={7} />: an account of Enoch's call, his preaching, his
vision of a God who weeps, and the taking up of a city called Zion. The ancient Enoch
literature was, at that moment, effectively unavailable — one obscure English translation
existed, and the Aramaic originals would not be found for another 120 years.

This series is Hugh Nibley's attempt to put those two bodies of text side by side.

## What Nibley is arguing

The series has an unusually clean thesis. Nibley proposes the Enoch material as a
test case that the usual debates cannot spoil. Arguments about the Book of Mormon
and the book of Abraham can always retreat to the question of original documents;
with Enoch there is no such retreat, because Joseph Smith never claimed to be holding
a manuscript. What remains is a comparison, and Nibley spends thirteen installments
making it — first establishing that the ancient sources were genuinely out of reach
in 1830, then working through the parallels: the Watchers and the giants, the
"secret combinations" and the oath-bound societies, Enoch's stammering tongue, the
wandering of the world's people, the weeping God, the cosmology, and finally Zion
itself.

## How to read it

Three things are worth holding in mind.

**It is apologetics, and it says so.** Nibley is writing for a devotional magazine
with a case to make. His scholarship is real — the citations of Charles, Milik,
Vaillant, Van Andel, and Koch are genuine and mostly accurate — but his
characterizations of other scholars' motives are polemical and often unfair. The
evidentiary layer and the rhetorical layer are separable, and the series is far more
interesting when they are separated.

**It is fifty years old.** Written in the 1970s, the series predates the full
publication of the Qumran Enoch material and a great deal of subsequent work. Nibley
complains repeatedly that the Aramaic fragments were being withheld; Milik's edition
appeared in 1976, in the middle of the run, and carried conclusions — notably a late
date for the Parables — that cut against parts of the argument. Where Nibley reports
"the latest news," the news is now half a century old.

**Parallels are evidence of something, but of what is the question.** Nibley
establishes the correspondences carefully and they are often genuinely striking. What
they demonstrate is the harder question, and readers will differ on it. He is aware of
this and argues the point directly; whether the argument succeeds is left to the reader.
