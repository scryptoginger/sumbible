Genesis — the title means "origin" — is the book of beginnings: of the cosmos, of humankind, of sin and death, and of the covenant family. It unfolds in two unequal movements, and the shift between them is one of the most striking in Scripture.

The **primeval history** (chapters 1–11) paints on the widest possible canvas: the whole world, all of humanity. Two creation accounts stand side by side — the ordered, seven-day liturgy of chapter 1 and the intimate garden narrative of chapter 2. Then comes the fall (chapter 3), the first murder (chapter 4), the long genealogies, the flood (chapters 6–9), and the scattering of peoples at Babel (chapter 11). Its themes are foundational: a creation pronounced good, the entry of sin and death, divine judgment always tempered by mercy, and a humanity that keeps reaching past its limits.

With chapter 12 the lens narrows abruptly — from the whole world to a single family. The **patriarchal narratives** (chapters 12–50) follow the Abraham cycle, the Jacob cycle, and the long Joseph narrative, with Isaac's story woven between. At their hinge stands the promise to Abraham (<VerseRef book="genesis" chapter={12} verse="1-3" />): land, descendants, and blessing — a blessing meant, the text insists, to reach "all the families of the earth." That promise is the thread the rest of the Bible pulls.

Genesis sets several convictions in place at the outset. It names God as the Creator of everything (<VerseRef book="genesis" chapter={1} verse={1} />), and it names humanity — male and female — as made in God's image (<VerseRef book="genesis" chapter={1} verse="26-27" />), a claim about the worth of every person. Its patriarchs are not polished heroes; the book is candid about their deception, favoritism, and fear. And it closes on a clear statement of providence: Joseph, sold into slavery by his brothers, tells them at the end, "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good."

Because of that opening promise and that framing of God, Genesis functions as the prelude to everything after it. The exodus, the covenant at Sinai, the monarchy of David, the prophets' hope of restoration — and, for Christian and Latter-day Saint readers, the New Testament and the Book of Mormon — all reach back to Genesis for their terms. It is the first room of the house; every other room opens off it.
