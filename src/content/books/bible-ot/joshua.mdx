Joshua opens the Deuteronomic History. The book picks up the narrative thread that Deuteronomy 34 left at Moses' death on Mount Nebo: the LORD now speaks directly to Joshua — Moses' commissioned successor — and gives the foundational charge for entering the land the LORD swore to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. The book's Hebrew title is <LangNote term="Yehoshua" script="יְהוֹשֻׁעַ" language="Hebrew" gloss="‘The LORD saves’ / ‘The LORD is salvation.’ The Hebrew title of the book, matching the central figure's name. The name was first installed at Num 13:16 where Moses changes Hoshea (‘salvation’) to Yehoshua (‘the LORD saves’ — adding the YHWH-element). The LXX-Greek transliteration is Ἰησοῦς (Iesous), the same name as the NT-Greek name of Jesus. Hebrews 4:8 plays on the lexical continuity (‘For if Jesus had given them rest, then would he not afterward have spoken of another day’ — the verse's ‘Jesus’ in the KJV refers to Joshua per Greek-name continuity).">Yehoshua</LangNote> ("the LORD saves") — the same name that the LXX transliterates as Ἰησοῦς (Iesous) and that the NT carries forward as the Greek-name of Jesus. The lexical-typological link between Joshua and Jesus is one of the OT-NT canon's clearest single name-frameworks.

The book unfolds in four major movements. **Entry and central campaign (Joshua 1-12)** opens with the LORD's commissioning of Joshua and the Jordan crossing on dry ground; the Gilgal circumcision, the Passover at Gilgal, and the captain-of-the-LORD's-host theophany mark the new generation's covenantal entry; the central campaign opens with Jericho's fall and the Achan-and-Ai narrative, continues with the Gerizim/Ebal covenant ceremony Moses had commanded (Deut 27), proceeds through the Gibeonite framework and the southern campaign with the sun standing still over Aijalon, and closes with the northern campaign and the king-summary list. **Land allotment (Joshua 13-21)** distributes the inheritance among the twelve tribes — nine chapters of structural-comprehensive tribal-territorial enumeration, with the Levitical cities and the cities of refuge installed within the framework. **Eastern tribes and Joshua's farewell (Joshua 22-24)** records the trans-Jordan tribes' return + their altar of witness controversy, Joshua's farewell speeches to the people, the closing covenant-renewal ceremony at Shechem, and Joshua's death — closing the book on the same site where the Gerizim/Ebal covenant had been ratified, with the framework of land-as-covenantal-realization fully installed.

The book's place in the canon is structurally substantive. Joshua opens the Deuteronomistic History (Joshua-Judges-Samuel-Kings on the Martin Noth framework — the four books as a unified historical composition operating under the Deuteronomic covenantal-evaluation framework). The book's narrative tests Deuteronomy's framework: the LORD swore the land to the patriarchs (Gen 12:7, 15:18-21, 17:8); the LORD installed the covenantal-conditional framework (Deut 27-30); Joshua narrates the entry into the inheritance under the framework's positive register. The narrative's structural-tension — the conquest's completeness vs the explicit notations that some peoples remained (Josh 13:1-7, 13:13; 15:63; 16:10; 17:12-13) — sets up the Judges narrative's framework of cyclical-apostasy-and-deliverance that the DtrH will develop.

The book's NT reception is concentrated and theologically substantive. <VerseRef book="hebrews" chapter={11} verse="30-31" /> reads Joshua 6 and Joshua 2 as paired-faith-paradigms within the Hebrews 11 cloud-of-witnesses framework — the faith by which the walls of Jericho fell, paired with the faith by which Rahab the harlot perished not with them that believed not. <VerseRef book="james" chapter={2} verse={25} /> reads Rahab as the faith-and-works-justification framework's paradigm-figure alongside Abraham. <VerseRef book="matthew" chapter={1} verse={5} /> includes Rahab in the messianic genealogy of Christ — one of four women named in the Matthean genealogy, all with irregular-by-conventional-measure backgrounds. <VerseRef book="hebrews" chapter={4} verse={8} /> plays on the Joshua-Jesus name-typology framework: "For if Jesus had given them rest, then would he not afterward have spoken of another day" — the verse's "Jesus" in the KJV refers to Joshua, and the framework reads Joshua's land-rest as not the eschatological-final-rest the Christ-event accomplishes. <VerseRef book="acts" chapter={7} verse={45} /> records Stephen's recap of Joshua's leading the people "into the possession of the Gentiles." Together the NT references compose a substantive reception that reads Joshua at the typological register (Joshua as Christ's name-prefigurement; Joshua's land-rest as the prefigurement of the eschatological rest; Rahab as the inclusion-of-the-marginal framework's first instance) while preserving the OT-narrative's own historical-prophetic register.
