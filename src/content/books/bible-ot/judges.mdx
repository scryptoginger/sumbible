Judges narrates the pre-monarchic period — the era between Joshua's death and the rise of Israel's first king. The Hebrew title is <LangNote term="Shofetim" script="שֹׁפְטִים" language="Hebrew" gloss="‘Judges,’ plural of shofet. The Hebrew root sh-p-t carries both judicial-adjudication and military-deliverance senses; the book's shofetim are primarily charismatic deliverers raised up by the LORD to lead Israel against foreign oppressors, not judicial-bench officers. The LXX renders Κριταί (Kritai, ‘judges’) with the same dual sense. The book's recurring formula ‘the LORD raised them up judges’ (Judg 2:16, 18) installs the term in its deliverer-sense as the book's structural vocabulary.">Shofetim</LangNote> ("Judges") — the plural of *shofet*, a word whose root carries both judicial-adjudication and military-deliverance senses. The book's *shofetim* are primarily charismatic deliverers raised up by the LORD to lead Israel against foreign oppressors, not judicial-bench officers. The book is the second volume of the Deuteronomistic History on Noth's framework (Joshua-Judges-Samuel-Kings) and the narrative-historical bridge between Joshua's generation and the rise of the monarchy under Samuel and Saul.

The book unfolds in three major movements. **The framework-installation prologue (Judges 1-3:6)** opens with the tribe-by-tribe incomplete-conquest summary at Judges 1 (echoing and extending the territorial-incomplete notations at Joshua 15:63, 16:10, 17:12-13), continues with the angel-of-the-LORD's covenantal-lawsuit at Bochim (2:1-5) — narratively executing Joshua's farewell warning at <VerseRef book="joshua" chapter={23} verse={13} /> — and culminates in the cyclic-pattern's programmatic statement at <VerseRef book="judges" chapter={2} verse="11-23" />: Israel does evil → the LORD's anger is kindled → oppressor sent → cry to the LORD → judge raised up → deliverance → rest during the judge's days → Israel returns to evil after the judge dies. The prologue's interpretive framework governs the rest of the book.

**The judge-cycles (Judges 3:7-16:31)** narrate the cycle's recurrent execution across multiple judge-generations. Six major judges receive extended treatment: Othniel (3:7-11, the model-judge whose cycle is the framework's compressed first instantiation); Ehud (3:12-30, the subversive-narrative deliverance from Eglon king of Moab); Deborah and Barak (4-5, the Canaanite-coalition defeat under Sisera + the Song of Deborah's poetic celebration); Gideon (6-8, the Midianite-Amalekite deliverance + the eventual ephod-cult notation); Jephthah (10:6-12:7, the Ammonite deliverance + the daughter-vow narrative); Samson (13-16, the Philistine deliverance + the Nazirite-vow framework + the eventual capture and final deliverance). Six minor judges receive brief notations: Shamgar (3:31), Tola and Jair (10:1-5), Ibzan, Elon, and Abdon (12:8-15). The Abimelech narrative (Judges 9) sits inside the Gideon-aftermath as a cautionary-anti-monarchy tale; Abimelech is not himself a judge but a self-appointed king whose failed three-year tyranny anticipates the dangers of the monarchic institution.

**The appendix-narratives (Judges 17-21)** record two episodes that sit outside the cyclic-pattern framework but reflect its theological consequences: the Micah-and-the-Danites narrative (17-18) reporting religious-syncretism and tribal territorial-relocation, and the Levite's concubine and Benjamite civil war narrative (19-21) reporting moral-collapse and inter-tribal warfare. Both appendix-narratives are framed by the recurring formula "in those days there was no king in Israel: every man did that which was right in his own eyes" (17:6, 21:25; with a shortened variant at 18:1 and 19:1). The formula functions as the appendix-narratives' interpretive key and as the book's structural-bridge to the Samuel-and-monarchy material that follows.

The book's NT reception is concentrated at one significant passage. <VerseRef book="hebrews" chapter={11} verse={32} /> includes four Judges-figures in the faith-paradigm cloud-of-witnesses: "And what shall I more say? for the time would fail me to tell of Gedeon, and of Barak, and of Samson, and of Jephthae" — names rendered in the Hebrews-author's Greek transliteration of the OT-Hebrew forms. The Hebrews-author's selection is significant: the four named figures include the major-judge whose cycle features the prominent failure-and-recovery dynamic (Gideon's ephod-cult notation, Samson's serial failures, Jephthah's vow-and-its-execution, Barak's reliance on Deborah). The Hebrews-author reads the four figures at the faith-register rather than the moral-evaluation register — a register-distinction the book of Judges itself does not formally make.

The book carries the sober-handling base-rate noted from Joshua forward — and elevated. The narratives of Jael driving the tent peg through Sisera (Judges 4:21 + the poetic celebration at 5:24-27), Jephthah and his daughter (11:30-40), the Samson-Delilah cycle (16), the Levite's concubine (19), and the Benjamite-Jabesh-Gilead resolution (21) all require sober-handling. SumBible reports the spectrum of historical-Christian-and-Jewish interpretive responses (heroic-deliverance / morally-problematic / ANE-context-required / canonical-canvas-recording-without-endorsing readings) without arbitration. The book's voice-register is sober-cyclical-narrative — the book reports Israel's recurring failure under the Deuteronomic covenantal-evaluation framework, with the cyclic-pattern's framework-installation at Judges 2:11-23 setting the interpretive key.
