1 Nephi opens the Book of Mormon, and it does so in a voice — the first-person voice of Nephi himself, a younger son who will become the founding prophet of the people who bear his name. Everything the rest of the book builds on is laid down here.

The first movement (chapters 1–7) is the **departure from Jerusalem**. Lehi, Nephi's father, receives a prophetic call and a vision warning that the city will fall (<VerseRef book="1-nephi" chapter={1} verse="6-15" />); obedient, he leads his family into the wilderness. Two return journeys interrupt the flight — one to obtain the brass plates, a scriptural record (chapters 3–4), and one to bring Ishmael's family along (chapter 7). The first of these gives Nephi the watchword that defines him: "I will go and do the things which the Lord hath commanded" (<VerseRef book="1-nephi" chapter={3} verse={7} />).

The second movement is **vision**. Lehi dreams of a tree of life (chapter 8); Nephi, wanting to understand his father's dream, receives a far longer vision of his own (chapters 11–14). It is among the densest prophetic passages in Latter-day Saint scripture, previewing the mortal ministry of Christ, a coming apostasy and restoration, and the gathering of Israel in the last days.

The third movement is the **journey itself** (chapters 16–18): eight years crossing the Arabian wilderness, the building of a ship, and the ocean voyage to a new land. A recurring symbol is the Liahona — a compass given by God that functions only according to the faith and diligence of those who follow it.

The book closes (chapters 18–22) with the family's arrival, the first quarrels that foreshadow the centuries-long division between Nephites and Lamanites, and Nephi's extended quotation of Isaiah (chapters 20–21, drawing on <VerseRef book="isaiah" chapter="48-49" />) to set his family's story inside the wider covenant history of Israel.

Two patterns established here govern the whole Book of Mormon. The first is the **exodus pattern**: a faithful remnant is led out of a doomed society to a land of promise. The second is the conviction that God matches every command with a means to fulfill it — and that personal revelation, available to Nephi as much as to his prophet-father, stands alongside prophetic authority rather than under it.
