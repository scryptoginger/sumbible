The Book of Moses opens the Pearl of Great Price, and it is the most extensive of Joseph Smith's restorations of biblical material — eight chapters that both parallel and reframe the opening of Genesis.

It begins with a chapter that has no biblical counterpart at all. **Moses 1** is a visionary prologue: caught up to "an exceedingly high mountain," Moses sees God face to face, withstands a direct confrontation with Satan, and is shown worlds beyond counting. The chapter sets everything after it in a cosmic frame — and a Christ-centered one, declaring that those worlds were created by the Son (<VerseRef book="moses" chapter={1} verse={33} />).

**Moses 2–4** then retell the creation and the fall. The narrative runs closely alongside Genesis 1–3 but adds material found nowhere in the biblical text: the creation presented explicitly as the work of the Father through the Son, an expanded account of Satan's rebellion in a premortal heavenly council, and the distinctively Latter-day Saint reading of the Fall not as sheer catastrophe but as a necessary step that made human growth and agency possible.

**Moses 5–6** follow Adam and Eve beyond Eden. Here sacrifice is instituted — and explicitly interpreted, in the text itself, as a similitude of the future sacrifice of Christ — and the genealogy runs forward from Adam toward Enoch.

The longest and most distinctive section is the **Enoch material** of chapters 6 and 7, again without biblical parallel. It records Enoch's prophetic call, his vision of the whole human family across history, and the founding of the city of Zion — a community defined in a single, much-quoted verse: its people "were of one heart and one mind, and dwelt in righteousness; and there was no poor among them" (<VerseRef book="moses" chapter={7} verse={18} />). That picture of Zion has shaped Latter-day Saint thought and aspiration ever since.

**Moses 8** closes with the days of Noah, leading toward the flood that Genesis takes up. Across the whole book, a few convictions recur: that the creation is the work of Christ, that the Fall opened the way to growth rather than only to ruin, and that the consecrated society called Zion is both an ancient memory and a standing summons.
